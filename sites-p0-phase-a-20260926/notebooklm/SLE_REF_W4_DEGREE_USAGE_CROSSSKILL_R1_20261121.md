## 4. Thirty-five ORIGINAL academic candidates

#### REF4-DEG-001 — very vs too
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2. **Register / mode:** neutral; adjective/adverb degree.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Very strengthens a description; too says a degree exceeds a relevant limit or is unsuitable for a purpose.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use very + adjective/adverb, but too + adjective/adverb (+ for someone / to-infinitive where appropriate). Ask what practical limit too refers to.
- **Independent illustration:** After filling the water bottle, Noura said, “It is very heavy.” Her younger brother replied, “It is too heavy for me to carry.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The same bottle can be described as heavy, but only the second speaker explicitly judges its weight against his ability to carry it.
- **Watch out or limits:** Too is not simply a stronger synonym for very. In positive contexts, “too kind” may be affectionate or exaggerated; infer attitude from context.
- **Learner self-check:** Can I identify the limit or problem that makes too more appropriate than very?
- **Search hints:** very, too, degree, excessive, adjective, too heavy.

#### REF4-DEG-002 — too ... to vs ... enough to
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; sufficiency and limits.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Too ... to signals a limiting degree; adjective + enough + to says the degree meets a relevant requirement.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Compare too + adjective + to + base verb with adjective + enough + to + base verb; identify the particular action and its conditions.
- **Independent illustration:** The narrow path was too slippery to cycle along safely, but the wider path was dry enough to use.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The two descriptions evaluate different qualities against different action thresholds; they are not automatic grammatical opposites.
- **Watch out or limits:** These frames express practical judgments, not universal measurements; changing the person, purpose or conditions may change the judgment.
- **Learner self-check:** What action is being judged, and what would count as enough for it?
- **Search hints:** too to, enough to, threshold, too difficult, sufficiently.

#### REF4-DEG-003 — enough with nouns and adjectives
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2. **Register / mode:** neutral; placement pattern.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Enough expresses the required amount or degree; its position depends on the word it modifies.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use enough + noun (enough chairs) but adjective/adverb + enough (quiet enough, quickly enough). Include for + person or to + verb if it clarifies the standard.
- **Independent illustration:** The hall had enough chairs, but the microphone was not loud enough for the visitors at the back.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Quantity (chairs) and degree (loudness) use different word order while answering the same basic sufficiency question.
- **Watch out or limits:** “Enough loud” is not the normal pattern for this meaning; enough can also stand alone in context (“That is enough”).
- **Learner self-check:** Is enough placed before a noun or after an adjective/adverb?
- **Search hints:** enough chairs, loud enough, quantity, adjective enough, placement.

#### REF4-DEG-004 — so + adjective vs such + noun phrase
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; emphatic degree.
- **Meaning or strategy:** So typically intensifies an adjective/adverb; such intensifies a noun phrase, often including an adjective.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use so + adjective/adverb (“so useful”) and such + (a/an) + adjective + noun (“such a useful map”); omit a/an for plural or uncountable noun phrases.
- **Independent illustration:** The guide was so clear that I finished quickly. It was such a useful guide that I shared it with my group.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The first frame modifies clear; the second frame modifies the noun phrase a useful guide.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not insert a/an before an uncountable noun: “such useful information,” not “such an useful information.” Some fixed constructions work differently.
- **Learner self-check:** Am I intensifying an adjective or an entire noun phrase?
- **Search hints:** so, such, a useful, so clear, such information, intensifiers.

#### REF4-DEG-005 — quite vs fairly vs rather
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1–B2. **Register / mode:** neutral; context- and variety-dependent.
- **Meaning or strategy:** These adverbs can moderate a gradable description, but strength and attitude depend on variety, adjective and context.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Inspect what adjective follows and the speaker’s stance. Fairly often suggests a moderate degree; rather can add surprise or an unfavourable nuance; quite is especially context-sensitive.
- **Independent illustration:** An editor wrote, “The first draft is fairly clear, but the conclusion is rather long.” A colleague replied, “The title is quite excellent.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Fairly clear is a moderate assessment; rather long hints at a concern. Quite excellent may mean completely excellent in some usage varieties, unlike quite good.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not publish a fixed numerical scale for these words. British and American English, gradability and intonation may change interpretations.
- **Learner self-check:** Could the evaluation or degree change with the adjective or English variety?
- **Search hints:** quite, fairly, rather, moderate, degree, British American variety.

#### REF4-DEG-006 — almost vs nearly
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; approximation.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Almost and nearly often indicate that something came close to a limit or was short of being complete.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use almost/nearly before relevant quantifiers, adjectives and verb phrases when they convey near-completion; check whether the final event actually occurred.
- **Independent illustration:** Nearly everyone joined the museum visit, but two students stayed at school. The coach almost missed the bus, yet boarded just in time.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Neither nearly everyone nor almost missed means all people joined or the coach actually missed the bus.
- **Watch out or limits:** The adverbs are often interchangeable but not in every idiom or collocation. They are not precise percentage labels.
- **Learner self-check:** What boundary was approached, and was it reached?
- **Search hints:** almost, nearly, approximately, close to, almost missed.

#### REF4-DEG-007 — hard vs hardly
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; form and meaning contrast.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Hard as an adverb can mean with effort or intensely; hardly usually means almost not.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Compare work hard (“use considerable effort”) with hardly work (“do almost no work”); check the verb and intended meaning.
- **Independent illustration:** Mina worked hard to finish the model, but her partner hardly worked on it at all.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Changing hard to hardly reverses the evaluation rather than simply softening it.
- **Watch out or limits:** Hard also serves as an adjective (“a hard test”); hardly has other uses such as “hardly ever,” already covered in the frequency reference.
- **Learner self-check:** Does the context mean strong effort or almost no activity?
- **Search hints:** hard, hardly, work hard, hardly work, adverb, hardly ever.

#### REF4-DEG-008 — only vs just: focus and position
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; scope and focus.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Only limits the people, things or actions included; just may mean only but also has senses such as a short time ago or exactly.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Position only close to the word being restricted when ambiguity matters: “I told only Amal” versus “I only told Amal.” Check the intended focus.
- **Independent illustration:** “I sent only the summary to the team,” said Rei. “I just sent the summary,” replied Ben, meaning he had sent it moments earlier.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The first excludes other documents; the second may refer to recency, not exclusivity.
- **Watch out or limits:** Sentence focus varies with stress and context. Just and only are not always replaceable; avoid claiming one universal fixed position.
- **Learner self-check:** Am I limiting the object/action, or describing when something happened?
- **Search hints:** only, just, focus, position, only the summary, just sent.

#### REF4-DEG-009 — especially vs particularly vs specially
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; emphasis and intended purpose.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Especially and particularly can single something out as notable; specially often highlights a specific purpose or design.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use especially/particularly to identify a standout case; use specially when a thing is made, prepared or arranged for a specific use.
- **Independent illustration:** The children enjoyed the exhibition, especially the room with moving models. One model was specially built for visitors who use wheelchairs.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Especially singles out a favourite; specially describes why a particular model was built.
- **Watch out or limits:** Specially and especially sometimes overlap in natural English, and particularly can be similar to especially; review the intended distinction, not a rigid ban.
- **Learner self-check:** Is this a highlighted example or a special purpose for an action?
- **Search hints:** especially, particularly, specially, purpose, emphasis, standout.

#### REF4-DEG-010 — much, far and a little with comparatives
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; comparative intensification.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Comparatives can be strengthened or softened to express the size of a difference.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use much/far/a lot + comparative for a substantial difference and a little/slightly + comparative for a smaller one: much better; slightly cheaper.
- **Independent illustration:** After the route changed, the second walk was much shorter, while the train fare was only a little cheaper.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The adverbs describe the size of the comparison; they do not say the walk was short in absolute terms.
- **Watch out or limits:** Normally say “much better,” not “very better.” Do not confuse comparative degree with the frequency of an action.
- **Learner self-check:** What is being compared, and how large is the difference?
- **Search hints:** much better, far cheaper, slightly longer, comparative degree, very better.

#### REF4-PRE-001 — interested in vs good at
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2. **Register / mode:** neutral; adjective + preposition.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Interested in expresses curiosity or engagement; good at describes competence. Their prepositions do not express the same meaning.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use be interested in + noun/-ing and be good at + noun/-ing. Check whether the sentence is about motivation or a skill.
- **Independent illustration:** Rafi is interested in astronomy, but he is especially good at explaining diagrams to younger children.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** An interest may exist without developed ability, and skill may exist without strong personal interest.
- **Watch out or limits:** Interest and competence can coexist. Avoid treating “interested at” or “good in” as interchangeable in these common patterns.
- **Learner self-check:** Am I describing what draws attention or what someone can do well?
- **Search hints:** interested in, good at, adjective preposition, interested in learning.

#### REF4-PRE-002 — listen to vs hear
- **Suggested navigation level:** A1–A2. **Register / mode:** neutral; attention and perception.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Listen to normally describes directing attention to sound; hear describes receiving or perceiving sound.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use listen to + sound/source and hear + sound/source with no to in the usual direct-object pattern. Contrast intention with actual perception.
- **Independent illustration:** Eli listened to the announcement carefully but could not hear the final sentence because a door slammed.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Paying attention does not guarantee successfully hearing every word.
- **Watch out or limits:** “Hear about” and “hear from” are additional valid constructions with different meanings. A reading-only reference is never evidence that a listening exercise was heard.
- **Learner self-check:** Did the person choose to pay attention, or perceive the sound?
- **Search hints:** listen to, hear, listen to music, hearing, object preposition.

#### REF4-PRE-003 — arrive at/in vs get to
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2. **Register / mode:** neutral; destinations and transport.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Arrive uses different prepositions depending on the destination, whereas get to can cover many ordinary destinations.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Typically arrive at a specific point/building, arrive in a city/country, or get to a place; with home, normally say arrive home/get home.
- **Independent illustration:** Our train arrived in Kyoto at noon. We got to the art centre an hour later and arrived home before sunset.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The examples contrast city, destination building and home without forcing the same preposition everywhere.
- **Watch out or limits:** Not every destination follows a mechanical size rule; established usage and context matter. Avoid “arrive to Paris” in this normal sense.
- **Learner self-check:** Is the destination a city/country, a specific point, or home?
- **Search hints:** arrive in, arrive at, get to, arrive home, transport.

#### REF4-PRE-004 — depend on vs rely on
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; relation and trust.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Depend on often states a condition or reliance; rely on commonly emphasizes trusting a person/system to provide help or function.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use depend on + noun/-ing clause as relevant (“depends on the weather”) and rely on + person/thing (“rely on the timetable”).
- **Independent illustration:** Whether the event goes ahead depends on the weather; the organisers rely on a clear forecast before deciding.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The first expresses a condition; the second explains what information the organisers trust for a decision.
- **Watch out or limits:** These expressions overlap in many contexts; they are not opposites, and “depend of” is not the normal English pattern.
- **Learner self-check:** Is this a condition, practical reliance, or trust in an agent?
- **Search hints:** depend on, rely on, preposition on, condition, reliance.

#### REF4-PRE-005 — afraid of vs worried about
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; emotional stance.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Afraid of commonly names fear; worried about commonly names concern regarding a person, possibility or outcome.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use be afraid of + noun/-ing and be worried about + noun/-ing. If a full clause is needed, choose a natural construction and preserve the meaning.
- **Independent illustration:** Ken is afraid of deep water; his sister is worried about tomorrow’s storm warning.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Fear of the situation and concern about a future event can overlap, but the phrase choice highlights different viewpoints.
- **Watch out or limits:** “Afraid that ...” and “worried that ...” also exist; these examples do not prohibit valid alternatives.
- **Learner self-check:** Does the sentence highlight fear or concern about an uncertain outcome?
- **Search hints:** afraid of, worried about, preposition, concern, fear.

#### REF4-PRE-006 — wait for vs expect
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; waiting and anticipation.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Wait for indicates remaining until an event/person arrives or happens; expect means believe or anticipate that something will happen.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use wait for + person/thing or wait to + verb; use expect + noun, expect to + verb or expect that + clause as appropriate.
- **Independent illustration:** We waited for the bus under the shelter. The driver expected it to arrive at half past eight.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Physical waiting and a belief about arrival are related but separate actions.
- **Watch out or limits:** Expect is not a substitute for wait in every context: “I am expecting the bus” may describe anticipation, not standing at a stop.
- **Learner self-check:** Is someone remaining until an event, or predicting/anticipating it?
- **Search hints:** wait for, expect, wait to, expecting, anticipation.

#### REF4-PRE-007 — discuss something vs talk about something
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; verb complementation.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Discuss usually takes the topic as a direct object; talk about uses about before the topic.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use discuss + issue/plan or have a discussion about + issue/plan; use talk about + issue/plan.
- **Independent illustration:** The class discussed the recycling plan yesterday and talked about ways to improve it today.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The subject is the same, but the verb patterns differ.
- **Watch out or limits:** “Discuss about the plan” is not standard for this transitive sense; “a discussion about the plan” is correct.
- **Learner self-check:** Is the word discuss a verb or is discussion a noun?
- **Search hints:** discuss, talk about, discuss about, discussion about, verb object.

#### REF4-PRE-008 — prefer A to B vs would rather A than B
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; preferences.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Prefer and would rather express a choice but require different structures.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use prefer + noun/-ing + to + noun/-ing; use would rather + bare verb + than + bare verb (or contextually omit the repeated verb).
- **Independent illustration:** I prefer cycling to driving on quiet roads, but on rainy days I would rather take the bus than cycle.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Prefer compares activities with -ing in this frame; would rather takes a bare verb.
- **Watch out or limits:** “Prefer to do ... rather than ...” is also possible; do not teach only one valid preference construction.
- **Learner self-check:** Which word determines whether the next verb takes -ing or a bare form?
- **Search hints:** prefer to, would rather than, prefer cycling, rather take, preference.

#### REF4-PRE-009 — look forward to + -ing vs want to + verb
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; forward-looking expressions.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Look forward to conveys positive anticipation; want to expresses desire. The to in look forward to is a preposition.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Use look forward to + noun/-ing, but want to + base verb. Check whether to is part of a prepositional phrase or infinitive marker.
- **Independent illustration:** The volunteers look forward to meeting the visitors. They want to prepare a welcome sign first.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The same word to introduces different structures; when the complement is a verb, the preposition requires its -ing form (meeting).
- **Watch out or limits:** “Look forward to meet” is not the standard complement here. Register can vary, and “I look forward to hearing from you” is a conventional closing.
- **Learner self-check:** Is to followed by a noun/-ing or a base verb in this expression?
- **Search hints:** look forward to meeting, want to meet, preposition to, gerund.

#### REF4-PRE-010 — used to + verb vs be used to + -ing
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; past habits and familiarity.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Used to + base verb describes a past state/habit; be used to + noun/-ing describes being accustomed to something.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Compare used to + verb (“used to walk”) with am/is/are used to + noun/-ing (“am used to walking”). Get used to + -ing describes becoming accustomed.
- **Independent illustration:** Before moving, Aya used to take the train; now she is used to cycling to school.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The first describes an earlier routine; the second describes present familiarity with a new activity.
- **Watch out or limits:** Negative/question forms of past-habit used to vary in usage and spelling. Do not assume every “used to” phrase marks a past habit.
- **Learner self-check:** Does the sentence describe a former routine or present adaptation?
- **Search hints:** used to do, be used to doing, get used to, past habit, accustomed.

#### REF4-WFN-001 — ask for information politely
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** polite email, message or request.
- **Meaning or strategy:** A polite request names the exact information needed and allows the reader to respond without guessing.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Identify the question, choose a fitting opening (Could you tell me ...? / I would like to ask ...), and add only relevant context.
- **Independent illustration:** In an invented email about a library event: “Could you tell me whether visitors need to register in advance?”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The request is specific and courteous; adding a generic “Please give me information” would be less actionable.
- **Watch out or limits:** An indirect question follows statement word order after its introductory phrase: “Could you tell me when it starts?”, not “when does it start?” Do not copy this into a live writing assessment as a ready-made answer.
- **Learner self-check:** Can the recipient identify the exact information I need?
- **Search hints:** polite request, could you tell me, registration, email, indirect question.

#### REF4-WFN-002 — clarify and confirm without inventing facts
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** polite clarification; neutral register.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Clarification asks what unclear wording means; confirmation checks an interpretation instead of declaring it true.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Name the uncertain detail, ask one narrow question, and mark any guess as a question rather than a fact.
- **Independent illustration:** In a fictional group-project message: “When you wrote ‘Friday afternoon,’ did you mean before 3 p.m.?”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The writer does not pretend that 3 p.m. is the agreed time; the message tests an interpretation.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not claim an agreement or changed deadline without evidence. Choose register according to audience.
- **Learner self-check:** Have I separated what was said from what I am asking someone to confirm?
- **Search hints:** clarification, confirm, did you mean, uncertainty, message.

#### REF4-WFN-003 — qualify a claim responsibly
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1–B2. **Register / mode:** neutral/academic; cautious stance.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Hedges indicate limits of confidence or scope rather than turning unsupported statements into facts.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Choose a qualification that matches evidence: may, can, seems, appears, in some cases, depending on the context.
- **Independent illustration:** For an invented opinion paragraph: “A later start time may help some students, although the effect could depend on travel arrangements.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The claim is a possibility with a stated condition; it does not assert a measured benefit or a percentage.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not add invented studies, numbers or quotes. “May” does not make an unsupported causal claim automatically valid.
- **Learner self-check:** What evidence do I have, and does my wording claim more than it supports?
- **Search hints:** hedging, may, seems, in some cases, qualify, cautious claim.

#### REF4-WFN-004 — recommend an action with a reason
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** neutral; suggestion, advice and justification.
- **Meaning or strategy:** A useful recommendation names an action and explains why it addresses a defined problem.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** State the practical issue, propose an achievable action, then give a relevant reason or expected benefit without overstating certainty.
- **Independent illustration:** For a fictional classroom notice: “I suggest placing a water refill station near the sports field because students spend long breaks there.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The statement connects action and location to an observed need; it does not promise a proven health outcome.
- **Watch out or limits:** A suggestion is not a command. If cost or accessibility matters, acknowledge constraints instead of pretending there are none.
- **Learner self-check:** Can a reader identify both my proposed action and why it fits the problem?
- **Search hints:** recommendation, I suggest, reason, action, justify.

#### REF4-WFN-005 — describe evidence without manufacturing numbers
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1–B2. **Register / mode:** neutral/academic; evidence integrity.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Writing should distinguish an observed example or a provided data point from a verified general trend.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Attribute a figure only when a real source supplies it; if no data are provided, write a bounded observation or hypothetical illustration as such.
- **Independent illustration:** In a fictional class discussion: “During our one practice session, several groups requested extra time.” The writer does not invent a schoolwide survey.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** A local anecdote can motivate a question but cannot prove what all students think.
- **Watch out or limits:** Never fabricate survey percentages, citations or expert statements. A reference example is not an approved answer to an active essay.
- **Learner self-check:** Could the reader tell what was actually observed and how broadly it applies?
- **Search hints:** evidence, observation, survey, invented statistics, attribution.

#### REF4-REA-001 — recognise problem–solution text structure
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** unscored reading strategy.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Some texts first identify a difficulty, then describe one or more responses and their limits.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** In an independent short paragraph, find the difficulty, proposed action and any evidence of whether it worked; do not assume a proposal already succeeded.
- **Independent illustration:** Invented mini-text: “The school garden dried out in summer. A group suggested collecting rainwater. The plan has not yet been tested.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The last sentence prevents reading the proposal as an established successful solution; this differs from simply tracking event order.
- **Watch out or limits:** Not all texts have a single solution, and paragraph order may vary. Do not infer outcomes absent from the text.
- **Learner self-check:** Which sentence states the problem, and is the proposed solution tested or only suggested?
- **Search hints:** problem solution, structure, proposed action, result, reading.

#### REF4-REA-002 — work out word meaning from nearby clues
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** unscored reading strategy.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Context can narrow an unfamiliar word’s meaning, but the correct sense must fit grammar and the surrounding idea.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Read the whole sentence and neighbouring explanation; propose a meaning and test it by substituting a simple phrase.
- **Independent illustration:** Invented mini-text: “The trail was muddy and slippery, so the hikers moved cautiously, taking small steps.” Here cautiously suggests with care.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** Action and consequences supply clues; matching a familiar word fragment alone is weaker evidence.
- **Watch out or limits:** Context does not guarantee an exact dictionary synonym. If uncertainty remains, mark it and consult a dictionary after the task permits.
- **Learner self-check:** Does my proposed meaning fit what people actually do in this passage?
- **Search hints:** meaning from context, cautiously, context clues, vocabulary in reading.

#### REF4-REA-003 — compare evidence across two short sources
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1–B2. **Register / mode:** unscored reading and source comparison.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Two texts may agree on facts while differing in interpretation, scope or stated uncertainty.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Identify each author’s specific claim, locate cited evidence, then describe agreement and difference without merging them into a made-up conclusion.
- **Independent illustration:** Invented extracts: Text A says the new footpath shortened one walk; Text B says it improved access but was closed after rain.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The texts address different outcomes; one does not erase the other, and neither proves access was always improved.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not invent a third source or treat two brief examples as a full systematic review.
- **Learner self-check:** Which claim belongs to each source, and what is still unknown?
- **Search hints:** compare sources, agreement, disagreement, evidence, reading.

#### REF4-REA-004 — distinguish a writer’s observation from evaluation
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** unscored reading strategy.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Texts may report an observable fact and then offer a judgment; readers should distinguish the two without assuming all opinions are false.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Mark what could be checked directly and which words express assessment; note whose viewpoint is represented.
- **Independent illustration:** Invented mini-text: “The exhibition opened at nine. The writer called its first room the most impressive.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The opening time is checkable; most impressive reports an evaluation, not a measured universal ranking.
- **Watch out or limits:** An evaluative sentence may be well supported; this strategy does not equate subjective with wrong or unimportant.
- **Learner self-check:** Could another informed observer reasonably evaluate the same event differently?
- **Search hints:** observation, evaluation, fact vs opinion, viewpoint, reading.

#### REF4-REA-005 — identify audience and communicative purpose
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** unscored reading strategy.
- **Meaning or strategy:** The intended reader and purpose help explain choices of information, structure and tone.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** Inspect the text type, action requested and distinctive wording, then infer a limited purpose supported by those cues.
- **Independent illustration:** Invented notice: “Please return the borrowed microscopes to the science desk by Friday so the next group can use them.”
- **Contrast or reasoning:** This is primarily an instruction/reminder to borrowers, not a report evaluating microscope quality.
- **Watch out or limits:** One text can have several purposes; do not over-interpret author identity, feelings or motives from one sentence.
- **Learner self-check:** Who is likely being addressed, and what action or understanding is invited?
- **Search hints:** audience, purpose, notice, informative persuasive, reading.

#### REF4-LIS-001 — identify speaker roles from spoken content
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2. **Register / mode:** unscored listening strategy; fictional scenario only.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Listening for what people say they need or can provide helps identify their roles in a conversation.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** On genuine verified audio, first ask who is speaking to whom; use stated responsibilities and the conversation context, not assumptions from voices.
- **Independent illustration:** Fictional teaching scenario (not an audio script): at a help desk, a visitor asks where a room is and another person explains the route.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The question-and-answer functions supply role evidence; voice pitch or accent alone does not establish identity or authority.
- **Watch out or limits:** This written scenario is not a real MP3 transcript or hearing evidence. For graded Listening, verified playback is required; otherwise UNASSESSED.
- **Learner self-check:** What actual words or actions in the audio would support each speaker role?
- **Search hints:** speaker roles, help desk, conversation, who is speaking, listening.

#### REF4-LIS-002 — distinguish a request, offer and instruction
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** unscored listening strategy; fictional scenario only.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Speakers can use questions and polite phrasing to request help, offer help or tell someone what to do.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** With real approved audio, attend to the intended action and who would perform it; wording and intonation both matter.
- **Independent illustration:** Fictional teaching scenario (not an audio script): one person asks for a map; another offers to show the entrance; a guide gives a safety instruction.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** These utterance purposes are different even though they all occur in one public-information setting.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not infer speaker intent solely from a single polite word or punctuation on paper. No assessment answers, audio keys or transcript are provided here.
- **Learner self-check:** Who is expected to act after the speaker finishes?
- **Search hints:** request, offer, instruction, communicative purpose, listening.

#### REF4-LIS-003 — notice uncertainty and probability in speech
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** unscored listening strategy; fictional scenario only.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Expressions such as might, probably, perhaps or I think can indicate uncertainty rather than a confirmed arrangement.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** When listening to a real clip, separate what the speaker confirms from what remains tentative; record uncertainty only after hearing the relevant wording.
- **Independent illustration:** Fictional teaching scenario (not an audio script): a coordinator thinks a meeting might move outdoors but has not announced a final location.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** A possible location is not the confirmed destination; this is different from a correction of a previously confirmed time.
- **Watch out or limits:** This reference never supplies a live task’s audio or answer. Missing/invalid playback keeps graded Listening UNASSESSED.
- **Learner self-check:** Which detail was certain, and which was presented as a possibility?
- **Search hints:** might, probably, perhaps, uncertain plan, listening.

#### REF4-LIS-004 — interpret approximate amounts and limits
- **Suggested navigation level:** B1. **Register / mode:** unscored listening strategy; fictional scenario only.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Words like about, at least, up to and no more than change whether a number is approximate, a minimum or a maximum.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** In verified audio, note the quantity and its qualifier together; re-listen to the permitted segment if you heard the number but missed the qualifier.
- **Independent illustration:** Fictional teaching scenario (not an audio script): a guide describes an event taking about twenty minutes, with no more than thirty visitors per group.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** About twenty is approximate; no more than thirty sets an upper bound. Neither implies an exact recorded count.
- **Watch out or limits:** Do not confuse “at least” with “at most.” This teaching scenario is not a transcript and does not reveal active audio answers.
- **Learner self-check:** Did the speaker give an estimate, a minimum or a maximum?
- **Search hints:** about, at least, at most, no more than, listening numbers.

#### REF4-LIS-005 — follow spoken stages without forcing keyword matching
- **Suggested navigation level:** A2–B1. **Register / mode:** unscored listening strategy; fictional scenario only.
- **Meaning or strategy:** Signposting language can help organise a sequence of actions, but the listener must still understand what changes at each stage.
- **Pattern or micro-procedure:** For genuine audio, listen for transitions such as first, next and finally; then paraphrase the actions rather than merely collecting these words.
- **Independent illustration:** Fictional teaching scenario (not an audio script): an organiser describes registering, checking equipment and then entering a workshop.
- **Contrast or reasoning:** The stages have functions; a list of signpost words without their associated actions would not demonstrate comprehension.
- **Watch out or limits:** This card is a strategy illustration, not pre-submit task content; supported full playback remains mandatory for a scored Listening attempt.
- **Learner self-check:** Can I explain the steps in my own words after actually hearing them?
- **Search hints:** first next finally, spoken sequence, stages, listening signposts.
