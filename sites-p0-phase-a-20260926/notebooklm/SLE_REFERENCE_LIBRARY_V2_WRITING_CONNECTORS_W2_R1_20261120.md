### REF2-LNK-013 — furthermore
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** formal
- **Function:** addition
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. Furthermore, + sentence B.
- **Meaning:** Adds another important supporting point in an argument.
- **Original example:** The new library is easy to reach. Furthermore, it has free study rooms.
- **Usage guidance:** Often begins a new sentence followed by a comma.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not join independent sentences with only a comma before furthermore.

### REF2-LNK-014 — moreover
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** formal
- **Function:** addition
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. Moreover, + sentence B.
- **Meaning:** Adds a second point that strengthens the previous one.
- **Original example:** The class project is inexpensive. Moreover, it uses recycled materials.
- **Usage guidance:** Usually followed by a comma at the start of a sentence.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not add it where the second idea merely repeats the first.

### REF2-LNK-015 — additionally
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** addition
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. Additionally, + sentence B.
- **Meaning:** Introduces an extra piece of relevant information.
- **Original example:** The workshop teaches photography. Additionally, students can practise editing.
- **Usage guidance:** Use a comma after an initial linking adverb.
- **Avoid / compare:** Not a direct conjunction between two clauses without suitable punctuation.

### REF2-LNK-016 — as well as
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** addition
- **Grammatical type:** complex preposition / coordinator-like expression
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** noun phrase + as well as + noun phrase (or -ing phrase)
- **Meaning:** Adds another comparable activity or item.
- **Original example:** The club teaches painting as well as drawing.
- **Usage guidance:** Often joins noun or -ing phrases; agreement may follow the main subject rather than the added phrase.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not automatically use it to join two independent sentences.

### REF2-LNK-017 — besides
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** informal
- **Function:** additional reason
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. Besides, + sentence B.
- **Meaning:** Offers a further, often conversational, reason.
- **Original example:** We can walk to the park. Besides, the weather is lovely.
- **Usage guidance:** Sentence-initial besides commonly takes a comma.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not confuse besides (in addition) with beside (next to).

### REF2-LNK-018 — in fact
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** clarification / correction
- **Grammatical type:** linking phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. In fact, + clarification
- **Meaning:** Adds precise facts that strengthen or correct an earlier impression.
- **Original example:** Some students expected a difficult quiz. In fact, it was quite short.
- **Usage guidance:** The second sentence should genuinely clarify the first.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use in fact to introduce an unrelated opinion.

### REF2-LNK-019 — on the other hand
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** two-sided contrast
- **Grammatical type:** linking phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** One side or advantage. On the other hand, + another side
- **Meaning:** Presents a different side of the same topic or decision.
- **Original example:** Online lessons are flexible. On the other hand, group discussion can be harder.
- **Usage guidance:** Use when comparing two meaningful perspectives.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use for every small contrast that is already clear with but.

### REF2-LNK-020 — nevertheless
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** formal
- **Function:** concession
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Unexpected obstacle. Nevertheless, + outcome
- **Meaning:** Signals an outcome that holds despite the preceding obstacle.
- **Original example:** The route was difficult. Nevertheless, the hikers finished safely.
- **Usage guidance:** Commonly sentence-initial with a comma.
- **Avoid / compare:** Avoid a comma splice between two independent clauses.

### REF2-LNK-021 — nonetheless
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** formal
- **Function:** concession
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Obstacle or limitation. Nonetheless, + contrasting claim
- **Meaning:** Signals that the following claim still holds despite a difficulty.
- **Original example:** The plan needs more time. Nonetheless, it deserves consideration.
- **Usage guidance:** Closely overlaps nevertheless; formality and context matter.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not imply there is a major obstacle when none has been stated.

### REF2-LNK-022 — whereas
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** contrast
- **Grammatical type:** subordinating conjunction
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Whereas + finite clause, + main clause
- **Meaning:** Compares contrasting facts within a complex sentence.
- **Original example:** Whereas my sister enjoys history, I prefer geography.
- **Usage guidance:** The opening whereas-clause is commonly followed by a comma.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use whereas + noun phrase in place of a finite clause here.

### REF2-LNK-023 — while
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** contrast
- **Grammatical type:** subordinating conjunction
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** While + finite clause, + main clause
- **Meaning:** Can contrast two facts or preferences.
- **Original example:** While I prefer printed books, my brother reads on a tablet.
- **Usage guidance:** While can also mean during the time that; context decides.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not assume every use of while expresses contrast.

### REF2-LNK-024 — even though
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** concession
- **Grammatical type:** subordinating conjunction
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Even though + finite clause, + main clause
- **Meaning:** Highlights a somewhat surprising contrast.
- **Original example:** Even though the bus was late, we reached the museum on time.
- **Usage guidance:** The initial subordinate clause is usually separated by a comma.
- **Avoid / compare:** Avoid 'even though ..., but ...' as a double connector in one construction.

### REF2-LNK-025 — as a result
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** result
- **Grammatical type:** linking phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Cause sentence. As a result, + outcome sentence
- **Meaning:** Introduces a consequence supported by an earlier cause.
- **Original example:** It snowed overnight. As a result, the match was postponed.
- **Usage guidance:** As a result of + noun phrase is a different construction.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not present an unsupported guess as a proven result.

### REF2-LNK-026 — consequently
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** formal
- **Function:** result
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Cause sentence. Consequently, + outcome sentence
- **Meaning:** Marks a logical consequence in a formal explanation.
- **Original example:** The measurements were inaccurate. Consequently, the experiment was repeated.
- **Usage guidance:** Often followed by a comma when used at sentence start.
- **Avoid / compare:** Not a substitute for however, which expresses contrast.

### REF2-LNK-027 — so
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** A1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** result
- **Grammatical type:** coordinating conjunction
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** independent clause + , so + independent clause
- **Meaning:** Joins a cause to its result within a sentence.
- **Original example:** The classroom was hot, so we opened the windows.
- **Usage guidance:** A comma is common when so joins two independent clauses.
- **Avoid / compare:** Avoid 'because it was hot, so we opened the windows' in a standard single construction.

### REF2-LNK-028 — for instance
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** example
- **Grammatical type:** illustrative phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** General point. For instance, + specific example
- **Meaning:** Introduces a particular illustration of a broader point.
- **Original example:** Some activities improve concentration. For instance, a short walk can help.
- **Usage guidance:** Often functions much like for example.
- **Avoid / compare:** An illustration is not proof that every case behaves the same way.

### REF2-LNK-029 — specifically
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** precision
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** General statement. Specifically, + precise detail
- **Meaning:** Narrows a broad statement to an exact detail or requirement.
- **Original example:** Our plan needs clearer steps. Specifically, we must agree on the deadline.
- **Usage guidance:** Use it when providing more precision, not necessarily a new example.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use it to introduce information unrelated to the general statement.

### REF2-LNK-030 — in particular
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** focus
- **Grammatical type:** linking phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** General statement. In particular, + highlighted item
- **Meaning:** Highlights one important member or detail within a broader set.
- **Original example:** Several clubs need supplies. In particular, the art club needs paint.
- **Usage guidance:** Can appear in other sentence positions with suitable punctuation.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use it to introduce something not included in the original set.

### REF2-LNK-031 — likewise
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** similarity
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. Likewise, + parallel sentence B
- **Meaning:** Signals a parallel action, feature, or outcome.
- **Original example:** The first group checked its sources. Likewise, the second group verified the facts.
- **Usage guidance:** Needs a meaningful similarity, not simply the next event.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not confuse similarity with cause-and-effect.

### REF2-LNK-032 — similarly
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** similarity
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Sentence A. Similarly, + comparable idea
- **Meaning:** Introduces something comparable in the relevant respect.
- **Original example:** Some students learn well from diagrams. Similarly, others benefit from visual examples.
- **Usage guidance:** Explain what makes the two cases alike when the relationship is not obvious.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use if the examples differ in the very feature being compared.

### REF2-LNK-033 — in contrast
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** contrast
- **Grammatical type:** linking phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Situation A. In contrast, + situation B
- **Meaning:** Directly highlights a difference between two things.
- **Original example:** The main road is busy. In contrast, the side street is quiet.
- **Usage guidance:** Choose comparable points of reference.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use if there is no real difference to contrast.

### REF2-LNK-034 — instead
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** alternative / replacement
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Rejected action. Instead, + chosen alternative
- **Meaning:** Indicates an alternative that replaces a previous possibility.
- **Original example:** We did not take the bus. Instead, we walked to the science museum.
- **Usage guidance:** May also appear before a verb phrase or at sentence end.
- **Avoid / compare:** Not interchangeable with however when nothing was replaced.

### REF2-LNK-035 — otherwise
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** condition / negative consequence
- **Grammatical type:** linking adverb
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Instruction or condition. Otherwise, + possible result
- **Meaning:** Means approximately 'if not' in this conditional use.
- **Original example:** Bring your student card. Otherwise, you may need to pay the full price.
- **Usage guidance:** Make sure the implied negative condition is easy to identify.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not treat otherwise as a general word for contrast.

### REF2-LNK-036 — unless
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** A2
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** negative condition
- **Grammatical type:** subordinating conjunction
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Unless + finite clause, + main clause
- **Meaning:** Normally expresses an exception: if the condition does not happen.
- **Original example:** Unless it rains, we will practise outside.
- **Usage guidance:** Unless it rains is similar in meaning to if it does not rain in this example.
- **Avoid / compare:** Avoid an unnecessary extra negative in 'unless it doesn't rain' when meaning 'if it doesn't rain'.

### REF2-LNK-037 — provided that
- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** B2
- **Register:** neutral to formal
- **Function:** condition
- **Grammatical type:** subordinating conjunction
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Main clause + provided that + finite clause
- **Meaning:** Adds a condition that must be met.
- **Original example:** You may borrow the camera provided that you return it tomorrow.
- **Usage guidance:** Often used when permission or agreement depends on a condition.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not mistake provided that for the past participle of provide in this construction.

- **Function:** summary / conclusion

- **Function:** priority / emphasis
- **Grammatical type:** linking phrase

- **Suggested level:** A2

- **Function:** sequence / initial priority
- **Grammatical type:** sequencing phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** First of all, + initial step
- **Meaning:** Signals the first step or first important point.
- **Original example:** First of all, read the instructions before using the equipment.
- **Usage guidance:** Useful when several ordered steps or points follow.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not pair it with an unrelated last point merely to sound organized.

- **Kind:** writing_connectors
- **Suggested level:** A1
- **Register:** neutral
- **Function:** sequence
- **Grammatical type:** sequencing phrase
- **Construction / punctuation pattern:** Action 1. After that, + later action
- **Meaning:** Refers to the next step after a previously described action.
- **Original example:** Mix the ingredients carefully. After that, pour the mixture into a tray.
- **Usage guidance:** Requires a clear earlier action or event.
- **Avoid / compare:** Do not use if the second event happened before the first.
