## 1. LIS-B2-02 — Follow the structure of an extended explanation
**CORE-1 — Goal and context.** You are helping a school club understand a talk about a trial for improving a shared space. After listening, identify the purpose, how supporting points are organised, a limitation and the next step. Focus on meaning rather than catching every word.

**CORE-2 — Learn the method.** On the first listen, identify the speaker's main question and rough stages (context → proposal → support → limitation → next step). On a focused listen, attach details to the stage they support. Signals like “however”, “for instance”, “the point is”, and “before we decide” can reveal structure, but do not treat them as automatic answer keys. A claim that something *may* help is not proof that it did. Separate what was observed from what is only proposed.

**Independent worked demonstration (reading about listening strategy, not assessed audio):** “The garden club wants to reuse pots. One volunteer has sorted the clean ones. However, the shed has no dry shelf. Before buying more pots, the club will check whether existing shelves can be rearranged.” Main goal: find a feasible reuse arrangement. Sorting is a supporting preparation detail; the dry shelf is a limitation; checking storage is the next step, not a purchase. A tempting wrong note is “Buy more pots” because it ignores “before”. This miniature model uses different facts from the assessed script. 
Learner-facing pre-playback situation only: A volunteer explains a possible improvement to a school club's shared space. First listen for the purpose. Then listen again for evidence, limitations and the proposed next step. Do not reveal the following transcript or answers until the assessment is submitted.

**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only assessed script (single speaker, original):**
> Last month, our school opened a small quiet room where students could spend part of the lunch break. The original idea was to offer an alternative to the crowded hall, especially for students who wanted to read or finish a small piece of work. We are now considering how to make the room more useful, rather than simply increasing the number of chairs.
>
> Let me explain what we noticed during the first two weeks. Several students used the room regularly, but the volunteer on duty said that newcomers sometimes stood at the door because they were unsure whether they needed permission to enter. That is an observation about how the room was used, not evidence that students disliked it. A clearer sign could help newcomers understand the basic arrangements.
>
> There is also a practical issue with space. The room has a narrow route between the shelves and the tables. Adding more chairs might make it harder for people to move around. Instead, we could try moving one table nearer the window, while keeping the route clear. The caretaker would need to check that the arrangement is safe.
>
> I should emphasise that neither suggestion has been approved yet. We also do not know whether a sign alone would change how students use the room. Before deciding, I would suggest asking the caretaker about the layout, then trying a temporary sign for a week if the school agrees. Volunteers could note whether newcomers still ask how to enter. That information would help us decide whether the sign should stay, or whether we need to explain the arrangements in another way.

## 2. LIS-B2-03 — Select notes that preserve evidence and limitations
**CORE-1 — Goal and context.** You are preparing notes for a library meeting after a report on a book-exchange trial. Distinguish a reported observation, an example, a limitation, and a conditional recommendation. Select useful details without transcribing every sentence.

**CORE-2 — Learn the method.** Use a compact note structure: claim/question | observed support | exception/limit | next action. Separate numbers from their denominators or context; a count of responses is not the whole population. When the speaker says “some”, “among those who replied”, “we cannot yet tell”, or gives an example, preserve the scope. Do not convert reported comments into measured outcomes. A good note is short but not less accurate than the original.

**Independent worked demonstration (different content):** “Eight people replied to a survey about an after-school drawing session. Six of those eight liked the time. We have not heard from students who could not attend. Ask them before changing the schedule.” Notes: six of eight respondents liked time; non-attendees absent; consult them before change. “Most students approve” wrongly changes the denominator and generalises beyond respondents. The example teaches the method, not the assessed facts.
Learner-facing pre-playback situation: A library coordinator reports on a trial. On the first listen identify the question being investigated; on subsequent listens choose notes that preserve the evidence and limits. Transcript reviewer/production-only until submitted.

**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only assessed script (single speaker, original):**
> We have finished the first month of a small book-exchange trial at the community library. The exchange is designed for people who want to pass on a book they have read and choose another one, without needing to buy anything. We wanted to find out whether the arrangement was easy to use, not whether it increased the amount of reading in the whole neighbourhood.
>
> We collected two kinds of information. First, the volunteers kept a record of books placed on the exchange shelf. They recorded forty-six additions over four Saturdays. That number tells us how often books were added. It does not tell us how many different people contributed, because the same person could bring several books. Secondly, we invited visitors to leave a short comment. Fourteen of the eighteen people who chose to comment said the instructions were clear. Two comments mentioned that the shelf was difficult to reach when the nearby reading table was busy.
>
> We must be careful about treating those comments as representative. Some visitors did not leave a comment, and we did not ask people who had never tried the exchange. In other words, the feedback can help us improve the shelf, but it cannot tell us what everyone in the neighbourhood thinks. One volunteer suggested opening another exchange point at the sports centre. That might be worth considering later, although nobody has checked whether the sports centre has suitable space or staff.
>
> For the next month, I would recommend keeping the existing shelf and testing a different position for the reading table, subject to the library manager's agreement. We can ask both users and non-users about accessibility. After that, we can decide whether an additional exchange point is justified. We should not announce an expansion before we understand the practical requirements.

LIS-B2-03-Q1 — listening.gist. What did the trial primarily investigate?
- A. The amount of reading throughout the neighbourhood. — Speaker explicitly distinguishes this from the aim.
- B. Whether all books should be sold. — The exchange does not require buying.
- C. Whether the existing exchange arrangement was easy to use. — **Key C.** This is the stated investigation.
- D. The cost of employing sports-centre staff. — No such cost study.

LIS-B2-03-Q2 — listening.detail. What does the figure forty-six refer to?
- A. Distinct contributors. — Same person could bring several books.
- B. Books added over four Saturdays. — **Key B.** Exact recorded quantity and context.
- C. Visitors who left comments. — Eighteen commented.
- D. Books read to completion. — Not measured.

LIS-B2-03-Q3 — listening.inference. Which note preserves the scope of the feedback?
- A. “All library visitors found the instructions clear.” — Extends beyond respondents.
- B. “14/18 volunteers approved the expansion.” — Wrong group and conclusion.
- C. “14 people confirmed reading increased.” — Wrong measured question.
- D. “14 of 18 voluntary commenters found instructions clear; non-users not surveyed.” — **Key D.** Correct denominator and limitation.

LIS-B2-03-Q4 — listening.detail. What problem did two comments mention?
- A. Difficulty reaching the shelf when the nearby table was busy. — **Key A.** Directly reported access issue.
- B. A shortage of books in the sports centre. — No sports-centre exchange exists in the report.
- C. High book prices. — No purchase required.
- D. Volunteers refusing to accept comments. — Not reported.

LIS-B2-03-Q5 — listening.speaker_intention. Why does the speaker mention an exchange at the sports centre?
- A. To announce its opening date. — No decision.
- B. To present a possible later option whose feasibility has not been checked. — **Key B.** Reflects suggestion plus uncertainty.
- C. To say it already has sufficient staff. — Staff availability unknown.
- D. To replace the library shelf immediately. — Speaker recommends retaining it.

LIS-B2-03-Q6 — listening.inference. Which recommendation best follows the evidence?
- A. Close the exchange because two visitors raised a problem. — Disproportionate.
- B. Open the second point immediately because forty-six books were added. — Count does not establish feasibility.
- C. Subject to agreement, test a layout adjustment and ask users and non-users before deciding on expansion. — **Key C.** Preserves conditional action and evidence gap.
- D. Claim that everyone in the area supports the exchange. — Unrepresentative data.

**CORE-4 — Repair and next action.** Q2/Q3/Q4: record the quantity, what was counted and whose evidence it represents. Q1/Q5/Q6: mark the aim, possible later option and conditional action. Replay post-submit and correct a note without turning uncertain claims into facts.
Unassessed independent extension, fresh context: Imagine receiving a report on a community noticeboard pilot. Build a note template separating observation, selected feedback, unrepresented groups and proposed next action. Explain why a voluntary comment cannot represent everyone. This is a strategy rehearsal, not evidence of hearing an absent recording.

## 3. LIS-B2-04 — Track speaker positions, qualifications and decisions
**CORE-1 — Goal and context.** You are helping a community committee prepare a neutral account of a discussion about a possible repair workshop. Follow two speakers' changing positions, distinguish agreement on a limited step from agreement on a full plan, and infer the evidence that matters to each speaker.

**CORE-2 — Learn the method.** Draw a two-column note grid, one column per speaker. For each, capture initial view, reason, concern, response to new information and final position. “I could support it if…” is conditional, not full agreement; “I see your point” acknowledges another view without necessarily adopting it. Listen for references and qualification across turns, not isolated keywords. A final plan may be narrower than the initial suggestion.

**Independent worked demonstration (different content):** “Nora: I'd like a weekly music club. Ken: I support music activities, but we don't know if a room is free every week. Nora: Then could we check for a single trial session first? Ken: Yes, let's ask about one session.” Notes: Nora shifts from weekly plan to a trial enquiry; Ken supports asking about availability, not weekly sessions. Agreement is on an enquiry only. Do not infer Ken dislikes music. 
Learner-facing pre-playback situation: Two committee members discuss an activity proposal. Listen for initial positions, reasons and the final decision. The following script is reviewer/recording-only until submission.

**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only assessed script (two voices, original):**
> Lina: I've been thinking about the unused corner of the community hall. Several neighbours have asked where they can get help with simple repairs, such as tightening a loose screw or replacing a missing button. I wondered whether we could run a monthly repair workshop. People might learn a useful skill as well as keeping an item for longer.
>
> Ben: I like the educational purpose, but I'm not ready to promise a monthly event. Last time the hall hosted a practical activity, the organisers needed someone who could supervise the equipment and make sure the space was left clear. We haven't established who would take responsibility for that.
>
> Lina: That's fair. I hadn't thought about the supervision arrangements. I wasn't imagining electrical repairs or anything involving dangerous equipment. We could start with ordinary clothes repairs and simple demonstrations using safe materials.
>
> Ben: That narrows the proposal, which makes it easier to discuss. Even so, I'd want to know whether there are volunteers with the appropriate skills and whether the hall manager is comfortable with the plan. We should also find out what residents actually want to learn rather than assume that the requests we've heard reflect everyone.
>
> Lina: Instead of asking the manager to approve a regular programme now, how about asking whether a single supervised demonstration would be possible? We could invite residents to suggest topics on a short voluntary form before planning the session.
>
> Ben: I could support that enquiry. If the manager agrees in principle, we can then check volunteer availability and the suitable materials. But we shouldn't advertise a date or invite people to bring items yet.
>
> Lina: Agreed. I'll draft a short message asking the manager about a possible demonstration. You can help me make sure it doesn't promise anything.
>
> Ben: Yes. The decision today is to ask, not to run the workshop. We can discuss whether a longer programme is worthwhile once we know more.

LIS-B2-04-Q1 — listening.gist. What is the main issue discussed?
- A. Whether to close the hall permanently. — Not the issue.
- B. Whether and how a possible repair activity could be considered safely. — **Key B.** Both speakers discuss a proposed activity and conditions.
- C. How to sell broken electrical equipment. — Not proposed.
- D. Which workshop date has been confirmed. — No date is confirmed.

LIS-B2-04-Q2 — listening.detail. What is Ben's initial concern?
- A. He believes residents do not enjoy learning. — No such belief expressed.
- B. He wants the hall to be used only for sports. — Not mentioned.
- C. He is unsure who would supervise the equipment and clear space. — **Key C.** He cites the unresolved responsibility from a previous practical activity.
- D. He says all volunteers have already refused. — Availability has not been checked.

LIS-B2-04-Q3 — listening.speaker_intention. What is Lina doing when she suggests ordinary clothes repairs and safe materials?
- A. Narrowing her initial proposal in response to Ben's concern. — **Key A.** She acknowledges supervision and limits activity type.
- B. Rejecting all suggestions from Ben. — She explicitly accepts the concern.
- C. Announcing approval for a different event. — Still a proposal.
- D. Promising that no supervision will be necessary. — A supervised demonstration remains the later suggestion.

LIS-B2-04-Q4 — listening.inference. Which description of Ben's final stance is best supported?
- A. Opposed to every repair activity. — He supports the educational aim and enquiry.
- B. Fully committed to a monthly workshop. — He repeatedly withholds that commitment.
- C. Uninterested in the manager's decision. — The manager's agreement is a condition.
- D. Open to a limited enquiry, with practical conditions still unresolved. — **Key D.** Preserves his conditional support.

LIS-B2-04-Q5 — listening.detail. What did Lina agree to do?
- A. Invite residents to bring items on a fixed date. — Ben explicitly says not to do that yet.
- B. Draft a message asking the manager about a possible demonstration. — **Key B.** This is her stated action.
- C. Buy new equipment. — Not agreed.
- D. Recruit a full team for a monthly programme. — Not authorised.

LIS-B2-04-Q6 — listening.inference. Which statement accurately reports the decision to an absent committee member?
- A. “A monthly workshop has been approved.” — Confuses an enquiry with approval.
- B. “The committee rejected the whole idea.” — Misstates conditional support.
- C. “They agreed to ask about one supervised demonstration; no date or programme is confirmed.” — **Key C.** Captures the narrow decision.
- D. “They verified residents' demand and volunteer availability.” — Both are outstanding.

**CORE-4 — Repair and next action.** Q2/Q3/Q4: identify who spoke, which concern was raised and how the proposal changed. Q1/Q5/Q6: distinguish purpose from actual commitment. Replay the final turns after submission and annotate a qualification.
