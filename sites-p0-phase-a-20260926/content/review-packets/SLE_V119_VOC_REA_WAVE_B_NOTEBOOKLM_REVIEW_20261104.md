## 2.1 VOC-A2-06 — Explain a reason and suggest a practical step (A2)

**Prerequisites:** VOC-A2-04, WRI-A2-05 · **Duration:** 24 min

### CORE 1 — Objectives
- Explain cause and result using because and so.
- Use natural chunks for practical recommendations.
- Distinguish a suggestion from an unsupported promise.
- Recall useful phrases and transfer them to a new situation.

### CORE 2 — Explicit teaching and independent demonstration
A useful recommendation identifies a problem, says why it matters, and proposes a manageable action. Because introduces a reason; so introduces a result. We make a suggestion, give a reason and take a first step. 'We could…' and 'One practical step would be to…' are appropriately polite. Could or might shows possibility rather than a guaranteed result. Avoid words like always unless you have evidence. After the questions, write one original two-sentence suggestion for a different situation; this is unscored self-practice.

**Optional Vietnamese explanation:** Đề xuất tốt gồm vấn đề, lý do và bước khả thi. Because chỉ lý do; so chỉ kết quả. Could/might không phải lời hứa chắc chắn. Tự viết hai câu mới để ôn thêm, không tự chấm năng lực viết.

1. MODEL · The class paper-collection boxes are hidden behind a cupboard. Because students cannot see them, some clean paper is thrown away. One practical first step would be to move the boxes beside the door. This could make recycling easier.
2. THINKING · The model names a specific difficulty, gives a cause, suggests a modest action and avoids an unsupported guarantee.
3. PITFALL · 'Everyone will recycle perfectly tomorrow' overpromises.
4. TRANSFER · Choose a different school routine. Name a problem, why it matters and one small step; unscored.

## 2.2 VOC-B1-06 — Qualify a claim and recommend a trial (B1)

**Prerequisites:** VOC-B1-04, WRI-B1-04 · **Duration:** 28 min

### CORE 1 — Objectives
- Use concession chunks to acknowledge a limitation.
- Distinguish evidence from a universal claim.
- Make a conditional proposal using provided that.
- Retrieve and apply two qualification phrases.

### CORE 2 — Explicit teaching and independent demonstration
A balanced recommendation gives a reason, recognises a limitation and suggests a practical next step. 'However, one limitation is…' introduces a drawback. 'Although…' introduces a concession; do not add but to the same although clause. 'Provided that…' makes a plan conditional. A short trial gathers evidence before a permanent decision. A small voluntary survey may suggest interest; it rarely proves that everyone will benefit.

**Optional Vietnamese explanation:** Đề xuất tốt thừa nhận giới hạn và điều kiện. Although không thêm but cùng cấu trúc. Provided that nghĩa là chỉ khi. Mẫu khảo sát nhỏ gợi ý ý kiến nhưng không chứng minh cho mọi người.

1. MODEL · A community greenhouse wants volunteers to water plants twice a week. A trial rota could reduce missed days. However, one limitation is that some volunteers travel during holidays. A sensible first step would be a two-week trial, provided that enough people sign up.
2. THINKING · The benefit is possible, the drawback is real, and the trial is conditional rather than certain.
3. PITFALL · 'Although people may travel, but the rota is perfect' doubles contrast and ignores uncertainty.
4. TRANSFER · Try a conditional recommendation for an unrelated community activity; unscored.

## 2.3 VOC-B2-01 — Use precise stance and evidence-aware language (B2)

**Prerequisites:** VOC-B1-04, VOC-B1-06 · **Duration:** 30 min

### CORE 1 — Objectives
- Identify degrees of certainty in reporting verbs.
- Qualify conclusions from a self-selected sample.
- Distinguish correlation from causation.
- Use concession language and propose a stronger follow-up test.

### CORE 2 — Explicit teaching and independent demonstration
An academic claim must match its evidence. Suggests and indicates signal a tentative interpretation; proves or guarantees require stronger support. 'This does not necessarily mean…' blocks an unjustified leap without saying success is impossible. A voluntary sample may be self-selected and unrepresentative. Two changes occurring together do not prove that one caused the other. A careful recommendation identifies a possible benefit, a rival explanation, what has not been measured, and a useful next test.

**Optional Vietnamese explanation:** Lời khẳng định học thuật phải theo độ mạnh bằng chứng. Suggests/indicates mềm hơn proves. Mẫu tự nguyện có thể thiếu đại diện. Hai thay đổi cùng lúc chưa chứng minh nguyên nhân. Hãy nêu giới hạn và phép thử cần tiếp.

1. MODEL · In a voluntary music-practice trial, 14 of 20 respondents said that a shared schedule helped them practise more regularly. This suggests some respondents found it useful. However, a small self-selected group and no comparison period limit the conclusion. A longer trial with comparable groups might provide stronger evidence.
2. THINKING · The number describes opinions from respondents, not measured increases in skill or proven causation.
3. PITFALL · 'Seventy per cent proves all students will improve' overgeneralises.
4. TRANSFER · Draft a cautious conclusion and one missing measurement for another fictional trial; unscored.

## 2.4 REA-A2-07 — Follow a plan when a notice is updated (A2)

**Prerequisites:** REA-A2-04, WRI-A2-05 · **Duration:** 26 min

### CORE 1 — Objectives
- Understand the purpose of two linked notices.
- Separate original information from updated details.
- Interpret deadlines and conditions accurately.
- Use text evidence without inventing extra rules.

### CORE 2 — Explicit teaching and independent demonstration
Read the first notice for its original plan. Then read the update, marking what changed and what stayed the same. Words such as by and before mark deadlines, while if and only if mark conditions. 'Moved to' changes a location; it does not necessarily change the registration deadline. Before choosing a response, locate the sentence that contains the evidence. A possible Monday collection is not a guaranteed one.

**Optional Vietnamese explanation:** Đọc thông báo đầu tiên để biết kế hoạch gốc, rồi so sánh bản cập nhật. Chú ý thay đổi, giữ nguyên, hạn chót và các điều kiện. Không tự tạo ra điều kiện chưa được nêu.

1. INDEPENDENT MODEL · Swimming lessons originally begin at 9:00 a.m. on Saturday. An update says the first lesson will begin at 9:30 a.m. in Pool B, while registration still closes on Wednesday.
2. THINKING · Changed: the time and pool; unchanged: Wednesday deadline. The update is not a reason to invent a new registration requirement.
3. PITFALL · A new location does not imply a different sign-up deadline.
4. UNSCORED TRANSFER · For another short notice, label each fact OLD, CHANGED or UNCHANGED.

## 2.5 REA-B1-06 — Separate a recommendation from evidence for it (B1)

**Prerequisites:** REA-B1-04, WRI-B1-04 · **Duration:** 29 min

### CORE 1 — Objectives
- Identify the writer's recommendation in an evidence-led article.
- Distinguish observed results from interpretation.
- Explain a limitation of a comparison.
- Infer a cautious attitude without overstating evidence.

### CORE 2 — Explicit teaching and independent demonstration
An article may describe results, suggest an explanation, recognise its limits and recommend a next step. Underline its main claim and mark which details support it. A small comparison can be useful, but it may not prove that one method always works. Ask whether the groups were comparable and whether something besides the tested feature could explain the difference. Retain the author's degree of certainty: 'might help' does not mean 'is always better'.

**Optional Vietnamese explanation:** Phân biệt kết quả quan sát, diễn giải và khuyến nghị. Kiểm tra tính công bằng giữa các nhóm và nguyên nhân khác có thể ảnh hưởng. Giữ mức chắc chắn giống tác giả.

1. INDEPENDENT MODEL · A library tested larger shelf labels. Ten adult visitors said they were easier to notice, but children were not asked. The librarian suggested another test with younger readers.
2. THINKING · Observation: ten adult opinions; limitation: children missing; reasonable next step: test a different group.
3. PITFALL · Ten satisfied adults do not prove all students will prefer bigger labels.
4. UNSCORED TRANSFER · In a new article, mark CLAIM, OBSERVATION, LIMITATION and NEXT TEST.

### CORE 3a — Full original assessment passage
**Title:** What makes a science-fair display easier to learn from?

> At Eastbrook School's science fair, visitors used to walk past rows of posters and read the explanations quietly. This year, a group of pupils proposed adding small models that visitors could try. The pupils believed that taking part in an activity might make scientific ideas easier to remember.
>
> Before changing the whole fair, the organisers tested two types of displays one afternoon. Twelve visiting families chose a traditional explanation poster; another twelve chose a small working model with a student volunteer. After using their display, each family answered two short factual questions. The poster group answered thirteen of its twenty-four questions correctly, while the model group answered twenty of twenty-four correctly.
>
> The numbers looked promising, but the comparison was not perfect. The families were not placed in groups at random: some chose the working model because they already enjoyed hands-on tasks. The volunteers also explained ideas in different ways. Because the trial lasted only one afternoon, nobody knew whether the visitors would remember the information a week later.
>

## 2.6 REA-B2-01 — Evaluate competing claims in a school policy proposal (B2)

**Prerequisites:** REA-B1-03, REA-B1-06, VOC-B2-01 · **Duration:** 32 min

### CORE 1 — Objectives
- Locate a qualified thesis in an extended article.
- Evaluate statistics using the correct denominator and likely response bias.
- Distinguish correlations from causal conclusions.
- Assess a fair policy recommendation that considers access and costs.

### CORE 2 — Explicit teaching and independent demonstration
At B2, critical reading means checking how an argument was constructed, not only finding facts. Identify the thesis, observation, interpretation, concession and proposed action. For a percentage, ask who was invited and who actually responded. If several things changed at once, a rise in outcomes does not prove any one change caused it. Look for omitted costs and groups that may have difficulty accessing a new system. The best conclusion fits the evidence and recommends an informative next test.

### CORE 3a — Full original assessment passage
**Title:** Digital screens or paper notices? A school decision under review

> When Northbridge School proposed replacing its printed event posters with digital noticeboards, the idea appeared straightforward. Screens could show last-minute timetable changes, reduce reprinting and let staff update several locations at once. The student council welcomed the proposal, while some families asked whether information might become less accessible.
>
> The school ran a voluntary three-week pilot with two screens in busy corridors. Staff continued to put up paper notices because they did not want pupils to miss events during the trial. At the end, an online survey invitation went to about 400 students; 62 replied. Fifty of those respondents said they preferred the new screens, a figure the council described as 'about four in five'. On its own, that sounds like broad support. Yet students who already liked digital announcements may have been more inclined to respond, and the school did not measure how many announcements were actually seen or remembered.
>
> During the pilot, sign-ups for two clubs increased. A newsletter called this proof that the screens had made communication more effective. That claim goes further than the available evidence. The clubs had also changed their meeting times and advertised through teachers. Without a comparable period or a reliable measure of notice exposure, it is impossible to tell how much influence the screens had.
>
> Other concerns were practical rather than ideological. A screen stopped working twice during short power interruptions. Staff reported that updating it was convenient, but they had not yet counted the time spent preparing digital graphics. In interviews, nine students who rarely used personal devices said they relied on wall notices when planning after-school activities. Digital screens could still help these students, but removing all printed alternatives before testing accessibility would be risky.
>
> The writer's position is neither to reject the technology nor to replace paper immediately. The school could keep one clear printed noticeboard while testing digital displays over a full term. It should record which messages reach students, how much staff time each format costs and whether different groups can find the information they need. If that evidence supports a broader change, the school would have a stronger case for making it.
