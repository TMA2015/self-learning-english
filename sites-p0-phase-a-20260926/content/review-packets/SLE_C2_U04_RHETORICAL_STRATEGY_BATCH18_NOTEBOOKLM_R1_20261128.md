## 1. REA-C2-03 — Evaluate rhetorical architecture and select audience-appropriate moves

**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Compare two sustained treatments of the same complex evidence. Explain how ordering, foregrounding, concession, understatement, metadiscourse and closing action influence what a reader notices or is asked to do. Select a revision that serves an explicitly defined audience without changing the evidential claim. Distinguish a persuasive stance from a factual distortion and a neutral account from an account with no evaluative purpose.

**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** Use two layers of a discourse plan. First list invariants: what was observed, the comparison limits, genuine counterconsiderations, decision authority and unknowns. Then map the rhetorical architecture: audience question → opening frame → order and relative prominence of reasons → placement and function of concession → metadiscourse that guides interpretation → closing action. Fronting a limitation may prevent misunderstanding; postponing it can be legitimate only if it remains recoverable before a reader must act. Understatement may soften a criticism but cannot make a material problem disappear. Concession acknowledges a real point; explain whether it restricts the proposal, changes its conditions or simply identifies a trade-off. Metadiscourse such as “the distinction matters here”, “this is a case for examining X, not deciding Y” or a signposted change of criterion guides interpretation, not proof. A neutral account may foreground a risk when that is the audience's decision question. Persuasion may openly recommend a bounded step while giving counterevidence enough prominence for informed judgment. Test revisions by asking what a reader could reasonably take away even without reading every sentence in isolation.

Independent worked demonstration (different topic): a fictional neighbourhood seed exchange records more requests after adding an online list, but has no record of requests made by people without internet access. An operations note opens with the missing comparison and proposes checking both channels; a proposal to the board opens with the benefit of knowing which seeds are available, then acknowledges the missing channel evidence before requesting a small comparison. Both can preserve the same facts. A headline “Online list reaches more people” changes the population-level claim; a final tiny footnote about missing evidence does not repair that headline. Rewrite the opening as a claim about recorded requests, put the limitation beside it, and make the next action a comparison rather than approval of replacing the counter. Explain how the two openings serve different readers without assuming that one audience is less capable of handling uncertainty. No active item or source fact reused.

**CORE-3 — Independent reading source (fictional).**

### Two documents about the same mobile repair service

Shared source notes: A community repair service tested a mobile van at two sites for four Saturdays, while keeping its usual fixed-site workshop open. Of 48 recorded van enquiries, 30 repairs were completed during the visit; the rest included requests needing parts or specialist advice. Volunteers recorded fewer enquiries at the fixed workshop than in the preceding four Saturdays, but the periods differed in local events and the recording method did not identify unique people. Several visitors at the van said they valued its location; the comments were volunteered, not collected from a representative sample. The trial did not measure cost per completed repair or compare access for people who did not attend. The committee may authorise a further limited trial; it has not authorised replacing the fixed workshop. The documents below are proposed communications based on these notes, not additional evidence.

**Document A — Proposal to the service committee, from a supporter of further testing**

[1] A repair service is useful only if people can make use of it. The mobile van has given us a reason to ask whether our current location serves everyone we intend to reach. Thirty repairs were completed among the 48 enquiries recorded at the van over four Saturdays, and several visitors volunteered that its location mattered to them. Those observations are a case for investigating a second route into the service, not yet a case for withdrawing the first.

[2] The fixed workshop recorded fewer enquiries than in the preceding four Saturdays. It would be tempting to describe the van as drawing people away from the workshop. That is more than the records allow: local events differed, and we cannot identify unique visitors across the two routes. Nor do we know the relative cost per completed repair. The apparent simplicity of a choice between two locations is therefore misleading.

[3] The immediate decision is narrower. I recommend one further, bounded comparison, with both routes retained, consistent recording, cost information and an effort to hear from people who did not attend. The van's early record is hardly a complete answer to our access question. But it is enough to make that question worth pursuing. We should decide whether to test the route, not decide in advance which route should survive.

**Document B — Neutral briefing for the coordinator designing that possible comparison**

[1] The current decision concerns whether to undertake a further comparison; replacement of the fixed workshop is not authorised. Four Saturdays of a mobile-van trial produced 48 recorded enquiries and 30 same-visit completed repairs. The recorded counts are not counts of distinct people. Some uncompleted requests needed parts or specialist advice.

[2] Fixed-workshop enquiries were lower than in the preceding four Saturdays, but the local-event schedules differed. Treat the apparent change in demand as a question to investigate rather than evidence that people transferred to the van. The comments on the van's location suggest a possible access benefit; because they were volunteered, they do not establish access for the wider community, including people who did not attend.

[3] Three kinds of information would be relevant to a comparison: comparable enquiry and completion records, costs relative to completed repairs, and access information from attendees and non-attendees. These measures answer different questions. A completed repair may indicate service output without showing whether a person would have been able to attend another site. A separate decision would be needed before changing either route permanently. This briefing sets out the questions for a possible test; it does not recommend choosing a route now.

REA-C2-03-Q1 — What best explains the different opening frames of Documents A and B?
- A. A opens with a measured comparison of access across the community, while B rejects access as a relevant consideration. — Neither document establishes such a comparison; B explicitly includes access information.
- B. A foregrounds the possible value of another access route to justify investigating it, while B foregrounds decision status and the data needed for a comparison. — **Key B.** The ordering serves advocacy for a bounded test and operational planning respectively, without requiring different facts.
- C. A presents the van as already approved to replace the workshop, while B attempts to reverse that approval. — A explicitly rejects deciding which route should survive; no replacement approval exists.
- D. Both openings perform the same function because both use the repair counts. — Shared data can support different rhetorical functions and intended actions.

REA-C2-03-Q2 — What is the function of the concession about lower workshop enquiries in A's second paragraph?
- A. It acknowledges a potentially important observation, then blocks an unwarranted explanation and reframes the decision as a comparison. — **Key A.** The observation remains relevant, but different local events and missing identity information limit interpretation.
- B. It establishes that maintaining both routes is already more expensive than using only the van. — No relative cost evidence exists.
- C. It dismisses the enquiry counts entirely because they do not identify unique visitors. — The counts still describe recorded enquiries; their limitations restrict inference rather than erase them.
- D. It presents local events as the confirmed cause of the decline. — A possible competing explanation is not a demonstrated cause.

REA-C2-03-Q3 — Which account best describes the understatement “hardly a complete answer” in A's final paragraph?
- A. It signals that access evidence is unnecessary once the repairs have been counted. — A explicitly requests access information.
- B. It retracts the recommendation because the author no longer sees a reason to investigate. — The next sentence retains the bounded recommendation.
- C. It makes the limitation salient without treating it as a reason to abandon a narrower enquiry. — **Key C.** Its force comes from the contrast with the following sentence and the limited decision framed around it.
- D. It allows the author to imply that access improvement has been conclusively demonstrated without saying so directly. — That implication would conflict with the rest of the text.

REA-C2-03-Q4 — Which statement best compares the metadiscourse and closing actions of the two documents?
- A. B's phrase “These measures answer different questions” establishes that the proposed trial will succeed. — It clarifies the purpose of measures, not their future results.
- B. Both documents call for immediate selection of the most effective route, but use different degrees of formality. — Both distinguish investigation from selection.
- C. A's final contrast makes evidence collection optional if the committee accepts the access principle. — Evidence collection is integral to the recommended comparison.
- D. A's “immediate decision is narrower” directs the committee towards a bounded recommendation; B's distinction among measures guides the design of a possible test without advocating a route. — **Key D.** Both use signposting to constrain how the evidence should be used; their intended actions differ.

REA-C2-03-Q5 — The committee wants a short public update for people who may need repairs next month. Which proposed opening meets that purpose and preserves the evidence and service status?
- A. “The van completed 30 repairs during four Saturdays. The usual workshop remains open; no replacement has been approved, and the committee is considering whether to compare the two routes further.” — **Key A.** It foregrounds service status for potential users, preserves the observed result and does not promise future van sessions.
- B. “The van has made repairs more accessible, so the workshop will remain open only until the new route is established.” — Converts possible access benefit into a demonstrated result and invents a transition decision.
- C. “The van's 48 users show that demand has moved away from the workshop, although the final timetable is not ready.” — Equates enquiries with unique people and asserts transfer of demand.
- D. “There is no meaningful information from the van trial; the committee cannot consider another comparison.” — Erases the recorded outputs and overstates the evidence limitation.

REA-C2-03-Q6 — An editor proposes replacing A's opening with “The van is reaching people the workshop could never reach” and retaining the original caveats in paragraph 2. Which response best repairs the overall framing while preserving A's legitimate advocacy?
- A. Keep the headline; a later caveat always neutralises a strong opening claim. — The source does not establish unique or previously unreachable users, and the first impression remains misleading.
- B. Frame the opening as an observed service output and a possible access benefit, place the evidence limit alongside that claim, and retain the recommendation for a bounded comparison. — **Key B.** Repairs the discourse-level takeaway rather than merely changing the tone; advocacy for further inquiry remains intact.
- C. Remove all reasons for the comparison so the proposal cannot be seen as persuasive. — A recommendation can be persuasive and accurate; suppressing relevant reasons would undermine the purpose.
- D. Change “could never reach” to “rarely reaches” but keep the claim about distinct users. — The records do not identify distinct people or establish frequency of earlier access, so the unsupported inference persists.

## 2. WRI-C2-02 — Reorganise a complex case for advocacy and neutral decision support

**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Construct two coherent extended documents using the same evidence but different rhetorical architecture, with an explicit purpose for each. Choose and justify the opening frame, order of reasons and counterconsiderations, concession placement, metadiscourse and final action. Repair a misleading discourse-level claim and explain how it would affect a reader. This goes beyond isolated lexical substitutions or a polite message/formal minute contrast. No C2 writing score is awarded.

**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** Plan Topic, Task, Must Include and Boundary. Create one invariant evidence ledger before drafting. Next outline two different routes through the evidence: a bounded advocacy argument can begin with the reason a proposal merits examination, confront the strongest counterconsideration before recommending a feasible next step, and mark the exact decision sought; a neutral design brief can begin with the decision question, organise the evidence by criterion and specify unknowns, separating observations from recommendations supplied by stakeholders. Do not merely replace a few words or add a perfunctory objection. Use signposting to mark the changing function of a paragraph. Preserve any material trade-off in both versions even if it receives different prominence. A revision can move a limitation closer to the claim, narrow a title or reframe an ending so the reader's likely takeaway is warranted. Do not claim that neutral writing must lack evaluation or that persuasion excuses factual drift.

Independent model (different topic, partial architecture only): a fictional school music room trial recorded fewer booking conflicts, while instrument condition and access for new users were not measured. An advocacy outline: purpose (fewer conflicts are worth examining) → observed record and its narrow scope → concerns about instrument condition and newcomers → request a second trial with measures. A neutral design outline: decision not yet made → booking record and limits → measures for condition and access → decision criteria and owner. Both must retain the missing access/condition information; neither can announce permanent adoption. A misleading title “Room now works for everyone” should become a title about recorded conflicts and a proposed check. This is a separate context and not a template answer for the source below.

**CORE-3 — Independent source bundle (fictional).**
- A community map project piloted two formats of a printed walking guide for six weeks at two community centres: a conventional route list and a diagram-led guide. The project aims to help visitors choose routes suited to their needs, not simply to reduce staff time.
- Staff recorded 36 requests for route clarification during the pilot, compared with 51 in the preceding six weeks. Visitor numbers and weather differed across the periods, and requests were not linked to the format received. No comparable measure of route completion, errors, confidence, or access for people who did not use either guide was collected.
- Eight visitors voluntarily commented on the diagram-led guide: five valued the overview; three said they still needed written details about crossings or surface conditions. These comments describe experiences among volunteers, not a representative preference estimate. A route-checking volunteer says keeping crossing/surface details accurate requires recurring checks. The team has not estimated the extra work for maintaining two formats.
- A designer recommends testing a combined guide with a diagram plus written details. A centre coordinator argues the current records cannot establish which format reduced clarification requests and wants a fair comparison and a maintenance estimate. Both support helping visitors choose appropriate routes. Neither has approved any format. The steering group alone may authorise a further bounded trial; no permanent publication or withdrawal of the route list is approved. Staff may prepare comparison criteria and an internal prototype for review.
- A draft promotional heading reads: “New diagram guide cuts confusion and works for more visitors.” It appears above the pilot counts without the comparison limitation.

## 3. C2-U04-TRANSFER-01 — Fresh rhetorical reframing for contrasting audiences

A fictional community science centre offered a trial of a self-guided activity booklet while keeping staffed demonstrations. In three weekend sessions, 24 of 32 recorded booklet returns included a completed observation page. The centre cannot tell whether these are distinct families, and it has no comparable return measure for the staffed demonstrations. Some visitors commented that they liked choosing their pace; other comments requested staff help with unfamiliar equipment. The comments were voluntary. The centre has not assessed learning gain, accessibility for non-visitors or the staff time needed to support both formats. The programme lead may propose a further comparison; only the centre committee may authorise it. No permanent change has been approved.

Task: write two compact documents of about 100–140 words each. First, a short recommendation to the committee advocating a further bounded comparison; second, a neutral update for families deciding what the centre currently offers. Preserve the narrow observation, comparison and sampling limits, and programme status. For the family update, avoid promising future dates or staff availability not supplied. Identify the intended action/need in each, explain two differences in rhetorical ordering, and revise one opening sentence that might otherwise make a possible benefit sound established. Self-check: same facts and limits, audience-appropriate prominence, no invented causal claim or approval, meaningful concession and closing action. No model answer or automatic score; weight 0; no audio/oral evidence or validated C2 Writing grade.
