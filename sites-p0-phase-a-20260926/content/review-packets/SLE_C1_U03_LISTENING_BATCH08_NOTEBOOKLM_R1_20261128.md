## 1. LIS-C1-01 — Trace a lecturer's argument beyond explicit signposts
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Follow a short extended lecture in which the speaker first presents an apparently attractive interpretation, then revises its scope. Distinguish the main proposition, illustrative counterexample, qualification and methodological implication. Make concise notes without turning a rhetorical setup into the lecturer's conclusion.
**CORE-2 — Learn the method.** Note what each segment *does*, not every word: introduces a tempting view; provides an example; exposes an assumption; qualifies; draws a limited implication. An example may be a counterexample rather than supporting evidence. Expressions such as “the difficulty with that account”, “what follows is not that…”, or “the distinction matters because…” can signal a change in argumentative function; meaning across sentences, not the marker alone, decides. Distinguish a lecturer's reported view from the view endorsed.
Independent written model (not assessed source): “A theatre's booking page lists every performance. It is tempting to call that equal access. Yet the page cannot tell us whether a visitor can reach the building. This does not make the page useless; it makes the claim about access too broad.” Note: listing coverage ≠ practical access; counterexample is travel; value retained; narrower claim. Common error: “The speaker opposes the booking page.” The speaker rejects the broad claim, not the tool. 
Learner-facing situation before audio: A lecturer discusses how a digital catalogue can shape interpretation of a collection. First listen for the line of argument; then note the role of examples and the limits of the conclusion. Do not expose the assessed transcript or answers.
**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only script, one voice.**
> When an archive makes its catalogue searchable, we tend to imagine that a previously inaccessible collection has simply become visible. That is a reasonable starting point, provided we remember what is meant by visibility. A catalogue can make a document much easier to locate; it can also determine which document is likely to be located in the first place. Those are related benefits and risks, rather than two separate technologies.
>
> Consider a fictional collection of notebooks from an amateur weather society. Some contributors recorded observations under the names of villages, while others used informal descriptions such as the fields beyond the railway. A search organised around current place names would make the first group of notebooks relatively easy to retrieve. The second group would not necessarily be missing from the collection, nor would it necessarily be less valuable. It would be less likely to appear in the results of that particular search. The distinction matters because a researcher could mistake an uneven route into the collection for an uneven distribution of historical observations.
>
> One might respond that a more comprehensive set of labels would solve the problem. It would certainly help. But there is a complication: a label that makes sense to a present-day reader may not faithfully represent the categories used by the people who produced the records. If we replace every ambiguous description with a confident modern equivalent, we may be making a decision on the researcher's behalf while appearing merely to improve the interface. That is not an argument for preserving every difficulty of the original material. It is an argument for making consequential editorial choices visible.
>
> Notice that the example does not establish how often such a distortion occurs in actual archives. It describes a mechanism by which it could occur. Measuring its prevalence would require a different investigation. For our purposes, the immediate question is whether a catalogue allows a reader to distinguish what was originally written, what an editor has inferred, and what has been left uncertain. A useful catalogue need not answer every historical question. It should make it possible to recognise which questions the catalogue itself cannot answer.
LIS-C1-01-Q1 — Which proposition best captures the lecturer's main argument?
- A. Searchable catalogues generally reduce the historical value of records. — Confuses discoverability with value.
- B. Modern terminology should be excluded from archive searches. — Speaker accepts its benefits.
- C. Search systems improve access while shaping discoverability; editorial interpretations and uncertainty should be visible. — **Key C.** Retains both benefits and the central qualification.
- D. Archive research is reliable only when original materials are difficult to locate. — Speaker explicitly rejects preserving all difficulty.
LIS-C1-01-Q2 — What is the rhetorical function of the weather-notebook example?
- A. Demonstrate a mechanism through which uneven search results could be mistaken for uneven historical evidence. — **Key A.** Illustrates an interpretive risk, not measured frequency.
- B. Establish that rural observers were less systematic than other contributors. — No such comparison.
- C. Prove current place-name searches usually omit important notebooks. — General prevalence not established.
- D. Recommend standardising the society's original observations. — Not the function of the example.
LIS-C1-01-Q3 — Why does the lecturer acknowledge that more comprehensive labels would help?
- A. To withdraw the earlier concern about search results. — Concern remains.
- B. To concede a practical benefit before introducing a distinct problem of interpretation. — **Key B.** A qualified concession, followed by an editorial risk.
- C. To imply that ambiguous descriptions are historically inaccurate. — Ambiguity does not imply inaccuracy.
- D. To show that the researcher should never inspect original wording. — Opposes the final recommendation.
LIS-C1-01-Q4 — What is implied by “making a decision on the researcher's behalf”?
- A. The researcher will necessarily accept every label as true. — Not established.
- B. Editors should leave all search terms undefined. — Not advocated.
- C. A confident modern label can conceal an interpretive choice by making it appear to be neutral retrieval. — **Key C.** Connects editorial inference with apparent interface improvement.
- D. A catalogue must reproduce each document in full. — No such requirement.
LIS-C1-01-Q5 — Which distinction does the lecturer make about the example's evidential status?
- A. It measures the frequency of distortion in actual archives. — Explicitly denied.
- B. It proves that every search interface distorts evidence. — Unsupported universal claim.
- C. It documents the historical weather society's actual search system. — Fictional illustration.
- D. It explains a possible mechanism; its prevalence would need separate investigation. — **Key D.** Preserves mechanism versus frequency.
LIS-C1-01-Q6 — Which note best preserves the final implication?
- A. Make original wording, editorial inference and unresolved uncertainty distinguishable; acknowledge catalogue limits. — **Key A.** Accurately captures the proposed principle.
- B. Maximise labels until uncertainty disappears. — Ignores irreducible uncertainty.
- C. Rank documents by historical value using search frequency. — Confuses discoverability with value.
- D. Avoid modern categories even where they clarify retrieval. — Too strong.

## 2. LIS-C1-02 — Infer the logic of challenges and concessions in a debate
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Follow a two-speaker debate on how to assess a programme. Reconstruct each speaker's underlying criterion, detect when a challenge concerns interpretation rather than the data, and identify a concession that narrows rather than abandons a position.
**CORE-2 — Learn the method.** Distinguish evidence, warrant (why evidence supports a claim), rival explanation and evaluation criterion. A speaker who accepts a datum need not accept the inference drawn from it. A challenge can move from “Did this happen?” to “What would count as success?” Track the point disputed in each turn. 
Independent written model: A club's organisers report 30 visitors to a demonstration. A supports repeating it because it attracted attention; B accepts the count but asks whether the aim was attention or acquiring a skill. A accepts that both outcomes require different evidence and still favours a small repetition. The disagreement is about a criterion, not arithmetic. 
Pre-audio situation: Two speakers discuss the evaluation of a volunteer mentoring programme. Listen for the criterion each speaker uses, how the disagreement develops, and what is actually conceded.
**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only script, two voices.**
> Asha: Our mentoring programme was meant to make the transition into the school less intimidating. The pilot attracted more applications than places, and most participants completed all four meetings. I would hesitate to redesign something before giving it a fair chance to develop. We have at least established that students were willing to take part.
>
> Daniel: I don't dispute that. What troubles me is the language in the draft report: it says the pilot improved students' confidence. Completing four meetings is compatible with feeling more confident, but also with feeling obliged to attend once a teacher has introduced you to a mentor. The problem isn't the attendance figure; it's the job we are asking that figure to do.
>
> Asha: Fair enough. The report should say participation was sustained rather than confidence improved. But I wouldn't treat the absence of confidence measures as a reason to suspend the meetings. If we insist on collecting perfect evidence before any activity can continue, the students who might benefit would lose the opportunity while we design our evaluation.
>
> Daniel: I'm not asking for perfect evidence. I am asking whether the programme might be evaluated in a way that gives the students who did not apply some visibility. If the participants were already comfortable asking for help, high attendance might tell us very little about reaching those who find the school hardest to navigate. A short confidential invitation to a wider group could explore that question without requiring them to identify themselves to the mentors.
>
> Asha: That changes how I'd frame the next stage. I'd still keep a small version of the existing programme available, but I would not describe it as a successful intervention yet. The wider invitation could inform whether the next version needs a different entry route. We should be clear that a response to the invitation would be evidence of a reported experience, not a direct measure of changed confidence.
>
> Daniel: I could accept that distinction. My remaining reservation is that we should set a review point before expanding the number of places. Otherwise an initially modest continuation can become a permanent commitment without anyone explicitly deciding it. If the report records the continuation as provisional, and states what is still unknown, I wouldn't oppose it.
>
> Asha: Then we agree on maintaining access at a limited scale while reconsidering the way we evaluate it. The decision about expansion remains open. Neither of us has evidence yet that mentoring caused an improvement in confidence.
LIS-C1-02-Q1 — What is Daniel's first substantive objection?
- A. The reported attendance figure is probably arithmetically wrong. — He accepts the figure.
- B. Attendance is being used to justify a claim about confidence that it cannot establish. — **Key B.** Challenges the warrant rather than the data.
- C. Students should be required to attend more meetings. — Not proposed.
- D. The original aim should be replaced with a numerical attendance target. — Opposite concern.
LIS-C1-02-Q2 — What does Asha's first concession change?
- A. Her support for offering any mentoring meetings. — She explicitly retains continuation.
- B. Her account of how many students participated. — Count not disputed.
- C. Her claim about the measured outcome, while retaining a limited practical case for continuation. — **Key C.** Sustained participation replaces improvement in confidence.
- D. Her view that no further evaluation is necessary. — Not her stated view.
LIS-C1-02-Q3 — Why does Daniel bring up students who did not apply?
- A. To argue that the current participants should be removed. — Not suggested.
- B. To assert that non-applicants have definitely been harmed. — No evidence.
- C. To propose replacing mentors with classroom teachers. — Not discussed.
- D. To question whether participation evidence addresses the intended reach of the programme. — **Key D.** The selection issue matters to the programme's purpose.
LIS-C1-02-Q4 — Which inference about Asha's final position is best supported?
- A. She accepts a narrower continuation and revised evaluation, not a claim of demonstrated effectiveness. — **Key A.** She distinguishes practical access from causal proof.
- B. She has abandoned the original aim of easing transition. — Unsupported.
- C. She now considers attendance data worthless. — She still accepts participation evidence.
- D. She regards wider invitations as direct measurements of confidence change. — Explicitly denied.
LIS-C1-02-Q5 — What concern does Daniel's final reservation add?
- A. Confidential invitations cannot produce any useful information. — Not his claim.
- B. The review should retrospectively remove the first four meetings. — Not proposed.
- C. A provisional continuation could turn into an unexamined permanent commitment. — **Key C.** Concerns decision governance, not simply outcome measures.
- D. The programme must expand before a review can occur. — Reverse of his condition.
LIS-C1-02-Q6 — Which summary captures the genuine common ground?
- A. They have verified confidence improvement but disagree on the expansion date. — Outcome unverified.
- B. They support limited provisional access and improved evaluation; expansion and causal conclusions remain open. — **Key B.** Both final qualifications retained.
- C. They agree to suspend all meetings pending a perfect evaluation. — Neither proposes this.
- D. They have decided participation alone is the success criterion. — The central disagreement rejects that inference.
**CORE-4 — Repair and transfer.** Replay after submission. Mark each challenge as data, inference, criterion, reach or decision. Q1/Q3/Q5: locate the exact target of a challenge. Q2/Q4/Q6: distinguish concession from capitulation, and supported conclusion from decision. Independent unassessed strategy practice: create an empty stance-change table for two speakers in a different debate on a school exhibition; list the kinds of evidence needed to distinguish participation from learning, without inventing claims from an unheard recording.
## 3. LIS-C1-03 — Interpret implied positioning and reconstruct a qualified conclusion
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** In an interview, identify a speaker's indirect resistance to a question's premise, distinguish a pragmatic qualification from a denial, and reconstruct the position without attributing an exaggerated claim. Use a note grid for attributed claims, implied limits, evidential status and decisions.
**CORE-2 — Learn the method.** An interviewer may frame an issue as a forced choice; a speaker can reject the framing rather than choose one side. Expressions such as “that is one way to describe it”, “I would put it differently”, or a question in return may politely challenge a premise. Infer the function using the full turn and subsequent clarification; tone alone is insufficient. Do not infer private motives. A qualified answer can acknowledge both a benefit and a cost, with a narrower recommendation.
Independent written example: Interviewer: “So the evening class failed?” Tutor: “That depends on what it was expected to achieve. It gave existing learners extra practice; it did not attract many new learners. I'd revise the invitation rather than describe the class as unsuccessful.” Note: rejects single success/failure frame; different goals, different outcomes; retains activity with revision. 
Pre-audio situation: An interviewer and an organiser discuss a trial that changed how students access feedback on their work. Listen for shifts in the question's framing and the organiser's evidence limits.
**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only script, two voices.**
> Host: You've moved part of the feedback process from written comments to brief consultations. The trial report says students asked fewer follow-up questions afterwards. Is that an indication that the consultations are simply more effective than written feedback?
>
> Organiser: It is one possible interpretation, although I'd put the comparison slightly differently. A written comment remains available when a student returns to a draft two days later; a conversation can allow the student to question a suggestion immediately. We were trying to understand when each form of feedback is useful. Counting later questions gives us a small part of that picture.
>
> Host: But surely fewer questions means there was less confusion. Otherwise why would you have introduced the consultations in the first place?
>
> Organiser: Fewer recorded questions means exactly that. A student may have resolved a difficulty during a consultation, but another may have decided not to raise a further question. We did not collect the same information about both students' reasons. I would rather not let the convenience of a count make that count a substitute for the thing we want to understand.
>
> Host: So are you suggesting the trial hasn't told you very much?
>
> Organiser: Not about a general improvement in understanding, no. It has told us something more specific. Several students who attended said that discussing one unclear comment helped them decide what to do next. Other students said they wanted to retain a written record so they could revisit the advice. Those reports are useful for planning a subsequent version, even though they do not tell us how many students improved their work as a result.
>
> Host: If both forms have a purpose, the obvious answer would be to offer every student a full consultation and a full set of written comments.
>
> Organiser: That might be desirable in an ideal timetable. The issue in this particular department is how to use the same allocation of staff time. We could test written comments with a brief optional clarification slot, especially for students who cannot identify a next step. I wouldn't promise identical access to individual consultations until we know what scheduling is feasible.
>
> Host: Then your position is that a consultation is a supplement rather than a replacement?
>
> Organiser: For the next limited trial, yes. I would not turn that into a general rule for every subject or every student. The aim is to see whether students can explain and carry out a next revision step, and to collect feedback from those who do not request a consultation. If we learn that the arrangement leaves some students with unresolved difficulties, that would be a reason to reconsider it.
LIS-C1-03-Q1 — What is the organiser doing in the first response?
- A. Challenging the single effectiveness comparison by distinguishing the functions of two feedback forms. — **Key A.** Rejects an oversimplified premise without denying a possible benefit.
- B. Accepting that consultations are always more effective. — Explicit qualification.
- C. Claiming written comments are unsuitable for any student. — Acknowledges their value.
- D. Refusing to discuss the trial findings. — Gives substantive interpretation.
LIS-C1-03-Q2 — What is implied by “let the convenience of a count make that count a substitute for the thing we want to understand”?
- A. All numerical data should be discarded. — Not the implication.
- B. The recorded count has been fabricated. — No claim.
- C. Students' understanding and recorded follow-up questions are not equivalent measures. — **Key C.** Separates convenient proxy from target outcome.
- D. The sole purpose of feedback is to reduce staff workload. — Unsupported.
LIS-C1-03-Q3 — How does the organiser respond to the host's suggestion that the trial has told them very little?
- A. By asserting a verified general improvement in understanding. — Explicitly declines.
- B. By treating every student comment as unreliable. — Recognises planning value.
- C. By arguing that only formal examinations can inform feedback. — Not stated.
- D. By distinguishing limited useful reported experiences from evidence of improved work. — **Key D.** Rejects all-or-nothing evaluation.
LIS-C1-03-Q4 — Why is offering everyone both full forms not adopted as the immediate recommendation?
- A. Written feedback has been banned. — False.
- B. The relevant constraint is the existing allocation of staff time and unverified scheduling feasibility. — **Key B.** Practical limit qualifies the proposal.
- C. Students have voted unanimously against consultations. — Unsupported.
- D. The organiser believes consultation is educationally worthless. — Contradicts reported benefits.
LIS-C1-03-Q5 — What limitation is placed on the label “supplement rather than a replacement”?
- A. It applies only if all students already understand feedback. — Not a condition stated.
- B. It describes a verified permanent policy for all departments. — Opposite.
- C. It is a working position for the next limited trial, not a universal rule. — **Key C.** Scope explicitly narrowed.
- D. It means that written comments alone have been proved superior. — No such proof.
LIS-C1-03-Q6 — Which note best captures the organiser's final position and evaluation plan?
- A. Reduce questions as the primary success measure and extend consultations everywhere. — Contradicts rationale.
- B. Continue both full services without reviewing staff time. — Not the proposal.
- C. Stop written comments and rely exclusively on interviews. — Opposite.
- D. Test written comments plus optional clarification within constraints; examine revision actions and feedback from non-users before wider claims. — **Key D.** Preserves proposal, outcome criterion and overlooked group.
**CORE-4 — Repair and transfer.** Replay question–answer pairs after submission. Q1/Q2/Q3: write the host's assumed premise separately from the organiser's actual answer. Q4/Q5/Q6: distinguish temporary trial from universal policy; keep constraints and evaluation target. Unassessed strategy practice: create an empty four-column table (question's premise / speaker's response / evidence limit / action) for a different interview about a community newsletter. No invented unheard content.
## 4. C1-U03-TRANSFER-01 — independent fresh listening synthesis
**Purpose:** after verified audio, reconstruct positions and important qualifications from a new recording. Qualitative self-review only, no score. 
Learner-facing task: Listen to a conversation about an oral-history exhibition. Write a 120–160-word neutral briefing that captures the initial disagreement, each speaker's criterion, one change in position, the evidence limitation and the proposed next step. Use your own words; preserve uncertainty. The word range is guidance, not a quality score. 
Reviewer/recording-only script (two voices):
> Mira: The exhibition includes short extracts from interviews with former residents. I'd like to organise the extracts around common themes rather than present each interview in its entirety. Visitors who have only ten minutes might otherwise struggle to make sense of the collection. The difficulty is deciding whether our arrangement helps visitors encounter the accounts or effectively tells them what those accounts mean.
>
> Leon: That's the distinction I wanted to raise. If we place two extracts under a label such as “nostalgia”, the label might lead visitors to interpret both accounts in the same way, even if one speaker was expressing a reservation about the past. I wouldn't oppose thematic organisation in principle. I would want the original context to remain accessible and the label to be recognisable as our editorial choice.
>
> Mira: I can see why that matters. The observation from the small preview was that visitors moved through the themed section more quickly. That might mean the organisation made navigation easier, but we did not ask whether they understood the speakers' different positions. It would be misleading to claim that faster movement meant deeper understanding. Perhaps the extracts could be presented under questions rather than interpretive labels.
>
> Leon: Questions could reduce the risk, although even a question can imply a particular way of reading an account. I would favour giving visitors a way to return to a longer segment, with a brief explanation of why the extract was selected. We should make sure the speakers' consent covers that presentation, rather than assuming permission for one format automatically covers another.
>
> Mira: Then let's prepare a small example of the question-led arrangement, subject to the agreed permissions, and invite a few visitors to explain how they interpreted the different extracts. We can compare that with a version that follows the interview order. I would not describe either arrangement as better before we have investigated the actual differences.
>
> Leon: That sounds like a worthwhile comparison. My support is for preparing and evaluating the examples, not committing the whole exhibition to a particular structure. If the comparison reveals that a concise arrangement loses an important qualification, we will need to revise the arrangement even if visitors move through it more quickly.
Post-submit self-check: Did I capture Mira's accessibility/navigation aim and Leon's interpretive-context concern? Did I distinguish faster movement from measured understanding? Did I preserve the proposed comparison, permission condition, and lack of commitment to an exhibition structure? Did I avoid claiming questions eliminate framing?
