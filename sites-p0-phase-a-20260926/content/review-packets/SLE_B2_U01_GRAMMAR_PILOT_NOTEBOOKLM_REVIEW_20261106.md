## CORE-1 — Meaningful learner goal

**Title:** *How sure are you? Modals of deduction in the present and past*  
**Module / CEFR teaching target:** Grammar / B2  
**Advisory duration:** 25–30 minutes, independent reading and practice

**By the end, you can:**
1. Choose `must`, `might/could` or `can't` to match the strength of evidence rather than guess what sounds confident.
2. Separate conclusions about now (`must be / might be / can't be`) from conclusions about an earlier event (`must have / might have / can't have + past participle`).
3. Recognise that `must` can express either a rule or a conclusion, and that a weak clue does **not** justify a strong claim.

**Why it matters:** When you interpret a school notice, discuss who changed a file, or evaluate a news claim, you need to distinguish evidence from assumptions. Careful language helps you sound precise, not merely certain.

## CORE-2 — Explicit explanation and independent worked demonstration

**Evidence strength and meaning**
- **Strong positive deduction:** `must be` (now), `must have + past participle` (earlier). This is a reasoned high-confidence interpretation, not logical proof.
- **Plausible but uncertain:** `might/may/could be` (now), `might/may/could have + past participle` (earlier).
- **Strong negative deduction:** `can't be` (now), `can't have + past participle` (earlier), when the available evidence rules out the claim.
- **Different function:** `must` can prescribe an obligation ('You must bring your library card'); `mustn't` usually prohibits an action ('You mustn't share passwords'). These are not interchangeable with `can't` as an evidence-based conclusion.

**Worked demonstration — NOT repeated in any graded question:**  
A classroom is empty at 15:15. There is a signed teacher log showing the last activity ended at 15:00, and the corridor camera shows all attendees leaving at 15:04. A careful conclusion is: *The group must have left before 15:15*. The conclusion refers to a past event; `must leave` would sound like a present rule, not a deduction. Without the log or camera, a closed classroom door alone would support only a possibility: *They might have left*.

**Reasoning demonstration:** (1) Identify the claim and its time. (2) Identify direct evidence, not just a plausible story. (3) Ask whether other explanations still fit. (4) Select the weakest modal that communicates the evidence honestly.

**Typical pitfalls:** Treating unanswered messages as proof nobody read them; saying `mustn't have` to mean 'impossible in the past'; and switching from the present to the past just because the evidence is recent.

### GRA-B2-01-Q01
**Question:** The library's electronic return record shows that the atlas was scanned back at 14:05. A message sent at 14:30 says it is still on loan. Which conclusion best fits the evidence?

A. The atlas must still be on loan.
B. The atlas can't have remained on loan at 14:30 if the return record is correct.
C. The atlas might be returned next week.
D. The atlas must not have been returned.

**Reviewer-only key:** B
**Why:** The earlier confirmed return contradicts the later claim that it remains on loan, assuming the record is accurate.
**Likely misconception:** Mistaking must not have for an evidence-based impossibility, or ignoring a timestamp.
**Next action after submit:** Compare the event times before choosing can't have + past participle.

### GRA-B2-01-Q02
**Question:** Mina's tablet is not in its usual drawer. Several students borrowed tablets today, but there is no sign-out record for Mina's device. What is the most defensible statement?

A. One of the students must have borrowed Mina's tablet.
B. Mina can't have moved her own tablet.
C. A student might have borrowed Mina's tablet.
D. The tablet must be broken.

**Reviewer-only key:** C
**Why:** Borrowing is one possibility, but the evidence does not identify a borrower or exclude other explanations.
**Likely misconception:** Treating a possible explanation as the only explanation.
**Next action after submit:** Before using must have, ask what alternatives remain plausible.

### GRA-B2-01-Q03
**Question:** During a live video call at 11:00, Luca shows the teacher a recognisable view from the sports centre. A classmate claims Luca is physically in the school laboratory at the same moment. Which deduction is justified?

A. Luca can't be in the school laboratory at 11:00.
B. Luca might have been in the laboratory yesterday.
C. Luca mustn't enter the laboratory.
D. Luca must have stayed home all morning.

**Reviewer-only key:** A
**Why:** The live-location evidence rules out his being in two different places at the same time; can't be expresses present impossibility.
**Likely misconception:** Using mustn't (prohibition) instead of can't (deduction) or changing present evidence into a past claim.
**Next action after submit:** Check whether the inference is about now or an earlier event.

### GRA-B2-01-Q04
**Question:** The school newsletter says: 'All visitors must wear a badge inside the building.' What does must express here?

A. A strong guess that visitors already have badges.
B. A rule requiring visitors to wear badges.
C. A conclusion about what happened yesterday.
D. A weak possibility rather than a requirement.

**Reviewer-only key:** B
**Why:** This is deontic must: an obligation established by the newsletter, not an epistemic deduction from clues.
**Likely misconception:** Assuming every must sentence reports certainty rather than a rule.
**Next action after submit:** Ask whether must comes from evidence or from an instruction.

### GRA-B2-01-Q05
**Question:** A school team was due to finish its meeting at 16:00. At 16:10 the door is closed and no voices can be heard, but the schedule says meetings sometimes run late. Which statement is appropriately cautious?

A. The team must have finished by 16:00.
B. The team can't still be meeting.
C. The team might have finished, although it could still be working quietly.
D. The team mustn't have met today.

**Reviewer-only key:** C
**Why:** The silence and schedule make completion possible, not certain; the meeting could have continued quietly.
**Likely misconception:** Inferring certainty from absence of noise, and confusing mustn't with can't have.
**Next action after submit:** Try to name one alternative explanation before choosing a strong modal.

### GRA-B2-01-Q06
**Question:** The photo archive has a verified timestamp of Tuesday at 09:00 and clearly shows the garden mural already complete. A student says the entire mural was first painted on Wednesday. What follows, assuming the timestamp is valid?

A. The mural might have been started on Thursday.
B. The mural must have been painted entirely on Wednesday.
C. The mural can't have been first completed on Wednesday.
D. The mural must still be wet.

**Reviewer-only key:** C
**Why:** An already-complete mural in Tuesday's verified photo rules out its first completion on Wednesday.
**Likely misconception:** Overlooking first and mixing a proven earlier state with a later speculation.
**Next action after submit:** Locate the exact claim contradicted by the earlier timestamp.

### GRA-B2-01-Q07
**Question:** Two teams could edit the presentation, but the revision history is unavailable. The finished slides contain charts that both teams know how to make. Which claim is justified?

A. Team A must have edited the charts.
B. Team B can't have edited anything.
C. Either team might have edited the charts; the evidence does not show which.
D. Both teams must have edited each chart together.

**Reviewer-only key:** C
**Why:** Both groups had access and ability, so either is possible; authorship cannot be determined from the charts alone.
**Likely misconception:** Treating capability or opportunity as proof of actual action.
**Next action after submit:** Distinguish access to a file from evidence of who changed it.

### GRA-B2-01-Q08
**Question:** A tutor says, 'The essay uses advanced vocabulary, so the writer must have understood the argument perfectly.' What is the strongest criticism?

A. Must have is wrong because it can only describe future events.
B. Advanced vocabulary alone does not justify certainty about understanding; might have is a safer claim.
C. Every modal of deduction must be followed by a present infinitive.
D. The sentence should use mustn't have because the writer used difficult words.

**Reviewer-only key:** B
**Why:** Lexical sophistication is not sufficient evidence of understanding; weaker epistemic commitment is more defensible.
**Likely misconception:** Confusing an observable surface feature with proof of a complex skill.
**Next action after submit:** Separate what the evidence directly shows from what it merely suggests.

### GRA-B2-01-Q09
**Question:** When the fire alarm sounded at 13:20, the office was empty. A verified log shows the final staff member signed out at 13:12. Which wording correctly makes a strong deduction about the earlier event?

A. The last staff member must leave before 13:20.
B. The last staff member must have left before the alarm.
C. The last staff member must be leaving tomorrow.
D. The last staff member mustn't leave before the alarm.

**Reviewer-only key:** B
**Why:** The reliable log supports a past deduction, expressed with must have + past participle.
**Likely misconception:** Using a present obligation form for a conclusion about a completed past event.
**Next action after submit:** Link the time of the evidence to the modal form.

### GRA-B2-01-Q10
**Question:** A student claims: 'We did not receive a reply by noon, so the organiser can't have read our message.' What is the best response?

A. Correct: lack of a reply proves the message was unread.
B. The organiser mustn't read messages before noon.
C. The organiser might have read it without replying; the original deduction is too strong.
D. The organiser must have deleted the message.

**Reviewer-only key:** C
**Why:** No reply does not rule out reading without responding; a possibility modal states only what the evidence allows.
**Likely misconception:** Treating missing evidence of one action as evidence that a different action was impossible.
**Next action after submit:** Test deductions against alternative explanations that fit the same facts.
