## 1. REA-C2-08 — Interpret humour, irony and pragmatic repair across audiences

**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Reconstruct the pragmatic trajectory of a written exchange in which irony directed at a process is received as possible criticism of a person. Distinguish the plausible readings at each stage from later clarification; identify changes in common ground across channels and select a repair that acknowledges effect, protects private context and resolves the practical issue.

**CORE-2 — Teach/model.**
- Separate literal proposition, possible implied stance, available contextual cues and recipient's uptake. An ironic utterance may depend on a shared contrast between wording and reality. Do not assume all readers share that contrast or the same relationship with the writer.
- Track the audience: a remark intelligible to a familiar group may become opaque or appear hostile when forwarded without its preceding turn, translated literally, or read by a new participant. A recipient's question is evidence of uncertainty, not proof of the writer's intention.
- Assess interpersonal risk independently of semantic interpretation. Repair by acknowledging the ambiguity or observable effect, clarifying the target of the criticism and naming the concrete issue. An explanation of intent does not erase an effect; an apology need not concede an unsupported accusation.
- Use private and public channels proportionately. A private clarification can invite discussion; if the ambiguity affected a shared decision, correct the shared record without repeating confidential details. Ask permission before sharing another person's private explanation. Do not treat a local preference as a universal cultural trait.
- When drafting, choose explicit, respectful wording and check whether the repair preserves any substantive objection. Avoid false harmony, excessive apology that changes the facts, or a demand that the reader accept a joke.

Independent worked model (different topic): a fictional volunteer theatre group repeatedly finds an empty prop cupboard despite a stock list. A long-time volunteer writes, “Our inventory has achieved invisibility again.” A newcomer receiving only that sentence asks whether their stocktaking is being mocked. Before clarification, the line could criticise the inventory mismatch or someone's work; its target is not fully explicit. The writer explains that the missing props, not the newcomer, were the intended target and acknowledges the ambiguity. A public repair says, “My comment was about the mismatch between the list and the cupboard; I should have made that clear. Let's check the two records before assigning any cause.” If the newcomer privately explains a particular language difficulty, do not disclose it without permission. The issue remains unresolved until records are checked. This model is not reused in practice.

**CORE-3 — Independent reading.** Read all four written records; consider what each participant could know at that time. No assumptions about national culture or actual speaker intent beyond supplied clarification.

### Reading asset SRC-REA-C2-08 — A kite workshop message travels between channels

[A] The familiar design subgroup is discussing an illustrated instruction sheet for a community kite workshop. The current sheet contains seven assembly diagrams but no indication of which steps require a helper. Three subgroup members have worked together for several seasons. A new illustration partner, Lian, prepared the diagrams from an outline supplied by the subgroup. The outline did not identify helper-dependent steps. No assembly trial with first-time participants has been conducted.

In the subgroup thread, before Lian joins it:
Mara: “We've somehow made the helper invisible in the instructions again.”
Dev: “Brilliant. Our diagrams have apparently discovered the self-assembling kite.”
Mara: “Let's mark the points where another pair of hands is needed before we try the sheet.”

[B] The coordinator forwards only Dev's sentence to the wider project channel, followed by “Can we revise the illustrations?” Lian reads it there:
Lian: “I am not sure whether 'brilliant' means the illustrations are approved or whether this is a criticism of my work. Could someone explain which change is needed? I used the outline as supplied.”
A colleague replies, “It's just Dev's usual humour. Everyone understands that here.”
Lian: “I didn't have the earlier discussion. I can make changes once I know which steps need a helper.”

[C] Dev sends Lian a private clarification:
“I was being ironic about the idea that the instructions could work without a helper, not judging the illustrations or your competence. I can see that the forwarded line didn't make that distinction. I should have stated the concern directly.”
Lian replies, “Thank you. I would prefer the correction to be in the project channel because that is where the revision request appeared. Please don't repeat my private explanation that I'm still getting used to the group's informal expressions. I'd rather discuss the technical question there.”
Dev agrees not to share that explanation.

[D] The coordinator confirms that the subgroup will identify the helper-dependent steps and Lian may then prepare revised diagrams. A closed assembly check is proposed; only the workshop lead may approve use of the sheet in a public session. Neither the check nor public use has been approved. Dev is drafting a reply for the shared channel. No record establishes how a first-time participant would interpret the diagrams.

- C. It proves the coordinator intended to embarrass Lian. — Motive is unsupported.
- D. It makes the ironic interpretation impossible for every reader. — Some may still infer irony; the cue loss raises ambiguity rather than eliminating all interpretations.

- C. It confirms that Lian's first interpretation was factually correct. — Lian asked which interpretation applied; the reply does not resolve it.

## 2. REA-C2-09 — Facilitate conditional agreement without erasing unresolved interests

**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Interpret a complex written negotiation involving differently situated contributors, an asymmetry of public visibility and distinct approval rights. Separate shared goals, procedural agreements, conditional consent and reserved decisions; choose diplomatic reframing that preserves each side's substantive concern and creates a realistic route for repair.

**CORE-2 — Teach/model.**
- Build an interest ledger: participant → stated position → reason explicitly given → acceptable condition → decision right → unresolved issue. Do not infer a hidden motive merely because a position is inconvenient.
- Distinguish four levels: shared aim, agreement to explore a process, conditional acceptance of an option, authorised final decision. The same words ("I can live with that", "in principle", "we can proceed") can refer to different levels. Name their object and conditions.
- A facilitator's paraphrase should be recognisable to each participant. Reframe accusations as the specific concern when the evidence supports it, but do not euphemise a material objection away. Acknowledge asymmetry: one participant may be visible in public while another bears responsibility for consent or implementation.
- Seek the narrowest genuine joint step rather than a mathematically equal split. If priorities cannot yet be reconciled, propose parallel inquiries or a contingent process. Record the unresolved issue and the person whose approval is required. Invite confirmation and a realistic reply window; silence does not automatically resolve a reservation.
- Calibrate tone with explicit conditions, not ornate politeness. Offer a private route for a sensitive clarification without requiring personal disclosures or assuming shared cultural norms. Written preparation for a spoken response is not evidence of speaking ability.

Independent worked model (different topic): a fictional puppet exhibition needs both visibility for volunteer makers and permission from a family lending an old puppet. The event organiser prefers prominent maker names; the lender accepts a temporary anonymous description but has not authorised a public name. Both agree to review two label drafts, not to publish either. A facilitator writes, “We agree that the contribution should be acknowledged and that the lender's naming choice must be respected. We can compare the anonymous and named drafts internally; public wording depends on the lender's decision and the curator's approval.” The first sentence preserves distinct concerns; the second offers an actual joint step without implying consent. If the lender later declines naming, revise the public option rather than portraying earlier internal review as assent. This independent model is not an active source.

**CORE-3 — Independent reading.** Read the full fictional correspondence and facilitator draft. Each contributor's preference is individual or explicitly tied to a local working practice; do not infer nationality or cultural identity.

### Reading asset SRC-REA-C2-09 — Who is represented in a shared cartography exhibition?

[A] A community cartography exhibition will display annotated maps developed with two neighbourhood groups. The working agreement permits internal drafting but requires each contributing group to approve the public description of its contribution. The exhibition chair alone approves the final layout after those confirmations. No final public description or layout is approved.

[B] Written planning exchange:
Exhibition designer Ellis: “I'd like a single collective credit on the entrance panel. The visitors should see that the map is a shared undertaking, rather than a competition over which group's annotations mattered more. We could identify individual contributions on the detailed labels.”
Neighbourhood liaison Asha: “I share the aim of presenting a joint project. A collective credit alone on the entrance panel would, however, make our group's involvement difficult to recognise at the point where the overall account is framed. Our members contributed annotations about routes that had been missing from earlier maps. We would need a visible acknowledgement of that contribution before agreeing to the public description.”
Neighbourhood liaison Bo: “Our group prefers not to be singled out on the entrance panel until the wording has been reviewed by members who weren't at the last meeting. I am not asking to remove Asha's credit. I am asking not to infer our approval from the fact that I am here. We normally give members several days to check public descriptions.”

[C] Later written exchange:
Ellis: “I can accept a short separate acknowledgement of Asha's group's route annotations within a collective heading, provided the heading still presents the map as a joint undertaking. We can leave Bo's group's proposed specific wording marked pending in the internal draft, not as a public blank.”
Asha: “That would address my concern in principle. I would like to see the exact wording before confirming it on behalf of the group. I don't require a decision from Bo's group today.”
Bo: “I can support preparing those alternatives for internal review. If members prefer a general collective acknowledgement of our contribution, please retain that option. I can arrange a three-day review and report the group's decision or any unresolved questions afterwards.”
Chair: “Prepare the two internal versions: collective heading with the proposed route acknowledgement and two alternatives for Bo's group's wording. No public layout decision is requested yet. The review period begins when both groups receive the actual draft. If we lack either confirmation after that period, we will record the pending issue and arrange a follow-up rather than assume consent.”

[D] Facilitator's proposed minute: “All parties now agree on a single entrance credit. Asha's group has accepted the wording, and Bo will approve it in three days. The chair can release the layout at the end of the review period.” Asha and Bo have not reviewed this draft minute.

REA-C2-09-Q1 — Which synthesis best represents the interests stated in [B]?
- A. Ellis wants to suppress the neighbourhood groups' contributions; Asha and Bo are competing for individual publicity. — Assigns motives and reverses Bo's explicitly different concern.
- B. Ellis values a collective framing, Asha wants visible recognition of a specific contribution, and Bo wants members to control the wording about their own group before public use. — **Key B.** Separates the three stated reasons rather than reducing them to personal rivalry.
- C. All three participants reject the collective framing of the project. — Asha shares the aim and Ellis proposes it.
- D. Bo's preference establishes that all neighbourhood groups require identical review customs. — A local practice cannot be generalised.

REA-C2-09-Q2 — Which interpretation of Asha's “That would address my concern in principle” is most accurate?
- A. It authorises public use of any acknowledgement the designer chooses. — The next sentence explicitly requires review of the actual wording.
- B. It withdraws the request for recognition of the route annotations. — The recognition remains the condition being discussed.
- C. It rejects the shared heading entirely. — Asha has accepted the combined approach in principle.
- D. It conditionally accepts the approach as addressing the concern, while reserving confirmation of the exact public wording. — **Key D.** Distinguishes agreement on a direction from approval of a text.

REA-C2-09-Q3 — What can a facilitator fairly infer from Bo's promise to arrange a three-day review?
- A. Bo commits to arranging the review and reporting its outcome or unresolved questions, not guaranteeing consent at its end. — **Key A.** Preserves process, authority and uncertainty.
- B. Bo guarantees that members will approve the separate credit. — The result is not known and two wording options remain.
- C. Bo has vetoed Asha's acknowledgement. — Bo explicitly rejects this interpretation.
- D. The chair can regard unanswered messages as approval after three days. — The chair specifies the opposite.

REA-C2-09-Q4 — What is the most appropriate diplomatic reframing of the remaining tension?
- A. “Asha wants special treatment, but Bo is more modest.” — Imputes personal traits and creates an unsupported comparison.
- B. “Both groups have agreed that the same wording must appear for them.” — Invents consensus and erases their different preferences.
- C. “We share a collective framing; the unresolved task is to recognise Asha's group's specific contribution while allowing Bo's group to choose its own public description after review.” — **Key C.** States common ground and both substantive interests without conflating them.
- D. “We should avoid any reference to credit so that no one feels uncomfortable.” — Erases the actual recognition concern and avoids the decision.

REA-C2-09-Q5 — Which process best matches the chair's authority and the participants' commitments?
- A. Publish the layout after three days unless either group objects publicly. — Conflicts with explicit confirmation rules and the chair's direction.
- B. Circulate the actual internal alternatives to both groups, record each confirmation or outstanding question, and seek final chair approval only after both confirmations. — **Key B.** Preserves the start of the review period, participation and separate approval.
- C. Ask Ellis to decide Bo's group's wording because the designer controls the layout. — Confuses design responsibility with consent.
- D. Hold Asha's group's review until Bo agrees to every aspect of Asha's contribution. — Bo is not demanding that authority; it creates an unnecessary dependency.

REA-C2-09-Q6 — Which minute repairs [D] while keeping the useful agreement?
- A. “There is agreement to prepare and circulate internal alternatives under a collective heading. Asha will review the precise route acknowledgement; Bo will organise a review of the group's own wording. Unresolved items will be followed up, and publication requires both groups' confirmations and final chair approval.” — **Key A.** Records the actual procedural agreement and conditional positions without inventing consent.
- B. “The groups failed to agree, so the joint project must be abandoned.” — Erases common ground and agreed drafting.
- C. “All agree to publish a collective entrance credit, subject only to a three-day waiting period.” — Substitutes time for substantive confirmation.
- D. “Bo is delaying the project because their group dislikes public recognition.” — Assigns unsupported motive and removes the stated review reason.

## 3. C2-U09-TRANSFER-01 — Facilitate a difficult written agreement and explain tone choices

A — Written discussion:

Contributor Sal: “The introduction should recognise the work of the growers who documented the seed histories. A generic thank-you might leave that work invisible. I am not asking for every grower to be named.”
Contributor Tavi: “I support recognition, but our group has not agreed to any public description identifying the people who supplied particular histories. Some members want to review the wording before deciding. I can organise the review; I cannot consent for those who haven't responded.”
Ren: “A shared introduction with a specific description of the documentation work might address Sal's concern without naming individuals. Let's draft that internally.”

B — Message copied into the shared thread from a familiar subgroup, without earlier context: “Splendid. We've invented a catalogue that documents its own history.” The writer, Ellis, later privately tells the facilitator that the irony concerned the omission of the growers' documentation work, not Tavi's review request. Ellis agrees to clarify that in the public thread. Tavi privately says the sentence seemed to criticise the request for review and asks the facilitator not to repeat that private message verbatim or disclose it without permission.

C — Subsequent stated conditions:
Sal: “A specific description of the growers' documentation would meet my concern in principle. I'd still like to see the wording.”
Tavi: “I'll circulate two alternatives: one naming the contributing group but no individuals, one using a general group description. Our members need four days after receiving the draft. I will report a decision or remaining concerns.”
Coordinator: “Internal drafting is authorised. No public catalogue is approved. If any confirmation is missing after the review window, keep the item pending and ask the relevant group for its status. Only then, once all confirmations exist, may I decide whether to approve publication.”
