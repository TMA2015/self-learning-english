## 1. LIS-C2-01 — Trace layered attribution and argument in an extended lecture
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Reconstruct an extended spoken argument whose apparent conclusion is successively reframed through quoted views, a counterexample, a distinction and a final synthesis. Attribute claims and keep the scope of a later qualification rather than treating a quoted proposition as the lecturer's own.
**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** Build a listening map: voice/source → claim → function → later revision → retained limit. First hear the trajectory; on replay note shifts in attributed speech, referents and what a contrast changes. A speaker may cite a compelling objection to expose its premise, not reject everything associated with it. Independent written model: a fictional textile exhibition curator quotes a visitor saying that a timeline makes the display objective, then distinguishes the order of dates from the selection of events. The chronology may be accurate; the selection still shapes interpretation. The quoted praise, the curator's distinction and the resulting recommendation belong in separate notes. This is not assessed audio. Neutral pre-playback orientation: a lecturer discusses an exhibition team's way of explaining a restoration project.
**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only script, one voice.**
> When people visit a restored building, they are often invited to notice the parts that have been returned to their original condition. There is a reassuring simplicity to that invitation, although the word original conceals a choice. Buildings have histories rather than a single birthday. One visitor may have in mind the day the building opened, another the period in which it became familiar to the neighbourhood. A later alteration can be historically significant without having been part of the first design. Let me use an imaginary restoration to illustrate why the way we explain such choices matters.
>
> The exhibition team has prepared two diagrams of a small assembly hall. The first depicts the hall at the time of its opening; the second identifies additions made during the years in which it served as a training centre. A member of the team objects: if we show visitors both diagrams, surely we will undermine the claim that the restoration is historically faithful. I think the objection deserves a better answer than simply insisting that more information is always beneficial. Two diagrams could indeed create confusion if neither made clear which period it depicted. But the uncertainty in that case would be a feature of the explanation, not an inevitable consequence of showing more than one period.
>
> There is another argument that sounds rather attractive. We might say that the building should be allowed to speak for itself. I would welcome that principle if it meant not dictating what visitors ought to feel. If, however, it meant presenting the restored hall without indicating which surviving features were retained and which were reconstructed, the apparent neutrality of the display would depend on visitors overlooking decisions that had already been made on their behalf. The absence of an explanatory label would not remove the interpretation; it would make its origin harder to identify.
>
> This is where we need to distinguish uncertainty about a historical fact from openness about its significance. The team may be able to establish when a partition was installed and still have good reason to leave open the question of which period is most meaningful to a visitor. Conversely, inviting different personal responses is no substitute for acknowledging that a particular construction date is uncertain. In neither case do we improve the exhibition by pretending the other kind of uncertainty has disappeared.
>
> My recommendation is therefore not to turn every label into a history of the entire building. Identify the period and evidence relevant to the restoration decision, mark any uncertainty in that evidence, and indicate how a visitor can examine the other period if it interests them. We can explain why a choice was made without suggesting that it is the only legitimate way to value the building. That, rather than the production of an apparently definitive account, is the kind of historical fidelity I would defend.

- C. It proves that the opening date of a building cannot be known. — The issue is significance and reference, not universal uncertainty about dates.
- D. It argues that visitors' memories are more reliable than documentary evidence. — No such comparison is made.

- B. Dismisses the objection because extra information can never confuse a visitor. — Explicitly rejected.
- C. Concedes that historical fidelity requires a single diagram. — Not the conclusion.
- D. Recommends omitting both diagrams because their dates are unverified. — Dates are not challenged in that example.

## 2. LIS-C2-02 — Recover pragmatic force and referents in compact exchanges
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Track a naturally brisk discussion in which abbreviated replies, reformulation, an interrupted proposal and a local repair change the action under discussion. Interpret pragmatic force using surrounding turns and later uptake; do not equate quick assent, hedging or tone with agreement.
**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** On replay reconstruct elliptical referents: what does “that”, “the other one”, or “as it stands” refer to at that moment? Separate agreement with a wording, a process and an operational decision. Record the original proposal, repair and remaining disagreement. Independent written model: A says “Can we send the schedule?” B replies “The outline, perhaps—not the times.” A says “So the outline only?” B says “For internal checking.” The abbreviated assent is to a restricted object and audience, not permission to publish. This written illustration is not hearing evidence. Neutral pre-playback orientation: three colleagues discuss a proposed community magazine issue. Listen for how their proposals and references change.
**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only script, three voices; conversational turn-taking, no overlapping answer-critical words.**
> Nia: We have enough material for the issue, at least on paper. Could we tell the contributors we're going ahead with the evening launch?
> Owen: With the issue, yes. The evening is a different matter.
> Priya: I thought the room had been reserved.
> Owen: A room has been held for us until Friday. Not necessarily that one, and not for the hours in the draft notice.
> Nia: Right, so we can put the notice out without the time—
> Priya: I'd rather we didn't call it a launch notice yet. People might take the word launch as a promise that there will be an event.
> Nia: Even if we say details to follow?
> Priya: Especially if we say that. It suggests the event is settled and only the details remain. Could we let contributors know that the editorial work is continuing? They needn't hear about a venue at all.
> Owen: That would deal with the immediate message. It doesn't quite deal with the practical question, though. The evening option assumes one of the volunteers can stay late.
> Nia: I wouldn't rule it out on that account. We haven't actually asked.
> Owen: Nor would I. I was objecting to treating the answer as already known, not to asking the question.
> Priya: Then an internal enquiry about availability, separately from the contributors' update?
> Nia: Yes, that makes sense. And if the room is confirmed we can send the launch notice?
> Owen: Confirmed for the right hours, and with a volunteer. Even then, we'd need the editor to approve the event. I'm not the person who can give that permission.
> Priya: Perhaps leave the notice out of today's action list. We can draft it privately, of course, but the action is to find out whether the evening is feasible.
> Nia: Fair enough. I'll change the heading on our task list from “confirm launch” to “check evening option”. Does the contributor message still say we're going ahead?
> Priya: With editing, yes. I'd make the object explicit this time.
> Owen: Agreed. We have enough material to continue editing. That's what we can say now.

## 3. LIS-C2-03 — Synthesise multiple spoken perspectives without false consensus
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Follow a moderated multi-speaker discussion in which participants share vocabulary but use distinct criteria, respond to objections asymmetrically and make a limited joint recommendation. Represent each speaker's rationale and the unresolved relationship between criteria.
**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** Build a comparison grid: speaker → criterion → evidence → response to another speaker → remaining condition. Identify where shared words conceal different meanings; a joint action can be justified by different reasons. Independent written model: a choir organiser calls a rehearsal accessible because the room is near transport; a participant means that the printed music can be read easily. Both support a trial but for different reasons. A summary must preserve both criteria, not declare access fully established. This is a written model only. Neutral pre-playback orientation: a moderated discussion examines how a community storytelling programme should evaluate a proposed change.
**CORE-3 — Reviewer/recording-only script, three voices plus moderator.**
> Moderator: The storytelling programme has been running in small neighbourhood groups. A proposal would bring some sessions together in a larger hall. Before we talk about a trial, I'd like to know what each of you would count as continuity. The word appears in all three of your notes, but I suspect you mean rather different things by it.
> Leena: From the point of view of the coordinators, continuity means that a group can still meet when its usual facilitator is away. At present, when a session is cancelled, the other groups can't readily offer a replacement because their rooms are small. The larger hall might allow a session to go ahead with a substitute. I'm not saying people would necessarily attend it. We haven't tried the arrangement.
> Tomas: For me the continuity worth protecting is the conversation itself. In the smaller group, someone can refer to a story told three weeks ago and expect the others to know what they mean. Bringing groups together might create new connections, which I welcome, but a larger meeting could also interrupt that shared thread. A record showing that the scheduled session took place would not tell us whether that continuity was preserved.
> Ruth: And I would distinguish both from continuity of access. A participant might know the group and be willing to talk, but not be able to reach the larger hall. The travel enquiry we ran asked people already attending sessions how they usually got there. It did not ask those who had stopped attending. I don't want an apparently reassuring set of replies to settle a question we didn't ask.
> Moderator: Leena, would you accept Tomas's point that a session can go ahead and still lose something the programme values?
> Leena: Entirely. I would still like to know whether we can avoid cancellations, but I wouldn't call the number of meetings an adequate success measure. In fact, I'd prefer to test a substitute arrangement in one existing group before assuming that bringing groups together is the way to achieve it.
> Tomas: That would make the question more manageable. I would not make a new facilitator reproduce every earlier conversation. I'd ask whether participants can pick up a topic they consider important and whether newcomers can join it. Those are two different possibilities, and continuity need not mean that nothing changes.
> Ruth: I could support investigating that smaller option, provided the enquiry includes people who haven't been coming recently. I don't yet have evidence about why they stopped. I wouldn't want the plan to assume it was travel, or assume it wasn't.
> Moderator: So there are at least three things to design for: whether a session can be maintained, whether conversations can develop, and who can actually take part. Can we agree to draft an evaluation plan for one existing group, including a way to invite views from non-attenders, and establish whether a substitute is available? A trial itself would need a separate decision.
> Leena: Yes, that's a useful first step.
> Tomas: Yes, provided the conversation measures aren't reduced to attendance.
> Ruth: And provided the enquiry doesn't presume the reason someone stopped attending.

LIS-C2-03-Q1 — Why does the moderator begin by asking what “continuity” means?
- A. To establish one dictionary definition before rejecting the others. — The discussion preserves different criteria.
- B. To reveal that a shared term masks different evaluation goals. — **Key B.** The opening structures the subsequent comparison.
- C. To obtain agreement on moving every group to the hall. — No such decision.
- D. To show that the three notes contradict the schedule. — No schedule contradiction is discussed.
LIS-C2-03-Q2 — Which distinction best represents Leena and Tomas's initial positions?
- A. Leena focuses on maintaining sessions despite absences; Tomas focuses on the development of shared conversations even if sessions occur. — **Key A.** Distinguishes logistical and conversational continuity.
- B. Leena wants permanent consolidation; Tomas opposes every new connection. — Both positions are overstated.
- C. Leena measures access through travel; Tomas measures facilitator availability. — Misattributes criteria.
- D. Both treat the number of meetings as sufficient evidence. — Tomas explicitly rejects this inference.
LIS-C2-03-Q3 — What is Ruth's concern about the travel enquiry?
- A. It establishes that travel is the main reason for every absence. — No reason for cessation is established.
- B. It invalidates all replies from current participants. — Their replies remain limited evidence.
- C. It samples current participants and therefore does not answer why others stopped attending. — **Key C.** Identifies the missing group and evidence boundary.
- D. It proves a larger hall is less accessible than every current room. — The enquiry does not establish that comparison.
LIS-C2-03-Q4 — How does Leena's later response change her initial proposal?
- A. She withdraws the aim of preventing cancellations altogether. — The aim is retained.
- B. She concludes that conversation continuity has already been demonstrated. — No such evidence.
- C. She accepts a merger on the condition that current attendance is unchanged. — She instead narrows the proposed investigation.
- D. She accepts that session counts are insufficient and proposes investigating substitution within one existing group. — **Key D.** Captures concession plus a revised means.
LIS-C2-03-Q5 — Why is Tomas's distinction between picking up topics and joining them important?
- A. It allows conversational continuity to include both existing threads and new participation, rather than requiring exact repetition. — **Key A.** He explicitly allows change while preserving a meaningful thread.
- B. It proves newcomers have already improved the meetings. — No observed result.
- C. It means only long-standing participants can assess continuity. — Newcomers are explicitly relevant.
- D. It reduces conversational continuity to a count of attendees. — Rejects that reduction.
LIS-C2-03-Q6 — Which account of the closing agreement is most faithful?
- A. All speakers now define continuity solely as maintaining scheduled sessions. — Criteria remain distinct.
- B. They agree to design a bounded evaluation and check substitute availability, with different criteria and conditions retained; a trial is not yet approved. — **Key B.** Synthesises shared action without erasing differences.
- C. They have approved a trial and confirmed a substitute. — Both remain pending.
- D. They agree travel explains the non-attendance and should be the sole measure. — Ruth explicitly rejects assuming the reason.
**CORE-4 — Feedback/repair.** After valid submission, replay and review each speaker's criteria.
