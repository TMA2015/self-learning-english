### 1. REA-C1-01 — Read between the lines: stance, evidence and qualified support
**Module / Target:** reading / C1; **Primary Framework Unit:** C1-U02; **Estimated:** 30 min; **Prerequisites:** REA-B2-01 (advisory, not locked).
**CORE 1 — Objectives**
- Identify a writer's qualified position in a demanding school-policy commentary without treating criticism as outright rejection.
- Distinguish observed figures, survey limitations, implied concerns and recommendations, citing precise wording.
- Explain how concessive and evaluative wording guides readers toward a cautious interpretation.
**CORE 2 — Teaching explanation, strategy and worked example**
CORE 1 — GOAL AND CONTEXT
Advanced readers distinguish the writer's overall stance from isolated negative comments. The context is a fictional school committee considering whether to continue a pilot project.

CORE 2 — STRATEGY
1. Identify the decision under debate and the author's position at the beginning and end.
2. Mark the boundary between facts (what was counted), interpretations (what the figures suggest) and recommendations (what should happen next).
3. Read concessions precisely: "encouraging without being decisive" acknowledges positive results while rejecting overconfidence.
4. Infer only what the text warrants. If a narrator reports that an editor rejected one headline, do not assume the editor rejected the entire article; distinguish the stated objection from an imagined wider motive.
5. When choices contain strong words such as "all", "never", "proves", or "should close", check whether the author made such a categorical claim.

WORKED DEMONSTRATION — DIFFERENT CONTEXT FROM PRACTICE
A fictional community group introduces a weekend bicycle rack. Its notice says: "Several cyclists have welcomed the new racks; nevertheless, their position near the entrance deserves another look." The writer supports the initiative in principle yet questions one design decision. A careful reader would NOT infer that the group wants the racks removed. The contrasting words "nevertheless" and "deserves another look" signal a limited reservation.

TYPICAL PITFALL
Reading one complaint as proof that a writer opposes an entire policy. Instead, identify which part is supported, which part is questioned, and how strongly.

UNSCORED TRANSFER
After practice, write a 70–100-word explanation of whether the Eastbridge writer supports the studio, quoting two distinct pieces of wording. This is optional self-practice, not scored reading evidence or a verified C1 designation.
**Independent examples:**
1. Independent model: A proposal for a weekend bicycle rack was welcomed, yet its entrance location needed review. The writer supports the plan conditionally; she does not demand that it stop.
2. Think-aloud cue: Ask whether a phrase states a fact, signals a reservation, reports a possible explanation or recommends a next step.
3. Self-check: Can you name one supported claim and one claim that the passage does NOT justify?
**CORE 3 — Original assessed passage PASS-REA-C1-01: Why the lunchtime studio should remain an experiment**
WHY THE LUNCHTIME STUDIO SHOULD REMAIN AN EXPERIMENT

When Eastbridge School converted a spare classroom into a lunchtime studio, the announcement promised a place where pupils could sketch, build prototypes or simply develop an idea without being graded. Its most enthusiastic supporters called it "a room for everyone". Six weeks later, the school committee is considering making the arrangement permanent. That is a reasonable possibility, but a premature conclusion.

The early figures are encouraging without being decisive. Forty-six pupils visited at least once; nineteen returned on three or more occasions. Those figures tell us that the room attracted interest. They do not tell us whether interest was widespread, whether regular visitors benefited more than occasional visitors, or whether pupils who stayed away had been offered a realistic opportunity to come.

A questionnaire completed by twenty-eight visitors was similarly positive: twenty-four described the studio as useful. It would be perverse to dismiss their experience. Yet the questionnaire, distributed inside the room, could scarcely reveal the views of pupils who never entered it. One need not be an expert in statistics to notice that omission. The headline "Most pupils support the studio", which appeared in an early newsletter draft, therefore goes further than the evidence allows.

Nor is the room's purpose as straightforward as its publicity suggests. Some visitors say that the chance to talk through an idea is its main advantage. Others appreciate somewhere quiet enough to concentrate. Both groups have a defensible claim on the space, but a single room cannot satisfy them simultaneously without some thought about how it is used. A notice asking everyone to "work creatively" does little to settle the issue.

None of this amounts to an argument for closing the studio. Equally, keeping the door unlocked is not quite the same as making the opportunity accessible. Pupils with lunchtime responsibilities or activities may never be free during the studio's advertised hours. The committee could try two quieter sessions each week, allow a limited number of supervised visits at another time, and invite feedback from pupils who have not attended. It could then examine not merely how many visits take place but whose participation changes.

Enthusiasm is worth preserving; it is not a substitute for inquiry. The choice is not between celebrating the project and obstructing it. It is between treating the pilot as a promising idea that can still be improved and treating applause as if it were the evidence that no improvement is needed.

### 2. VOC-C2-01 — Fine shades of meaning: tact, emphasis and qualified judgment
**Module / Target:** vocabulary / C2; **Primary Framework Unit:** C2-U01; **Estimated:** 30 min; **Prerequisites:** VOC-B2-01 (advisory, not locked).
**CORE 1 — Objectives**
- Distinguish close but non-equivalent statements by degree of certainty, concession, emphasis and interpersonal force.
- Select formulations that preserve a speaker's intended stance without increasing or reducing the strength of a claim.
- Recognise when subtle understatement, partial concession and tactful disagreement are appropriate to context.
**CORE 2 — Teaching explanation, strategy and worked example**
CORE 1 — GOAL AND CONTEXT
C2-level precision is not about collecting rare idioms. It involves choosing a phrase whose implication, emphasis and tone fit a specific audience and situation. These are advanced language targets, not a promise that one lesson establishes C2 ability.

CORE 2 — MEANING AND PRAGMATIC FORCE
1. Distinguish *degree*: "not entirely convinced" expresses a reservation, not complete rejection.
2. Distinguish *scope*: "some of the evidence" is not interchangeable with "all the evidence".
3. Distinguish *concession*: "not without merit" offers cautious credit while leaving space for objections.
4. Distinguish *foregrounding*: "not so much X as Y" places the emphasis on Y; it need not declare X irrelevant.
5. Distinguish *interpersonal effect*: a diplomatic correction may convey disagreement as accurately as a blunt one, but with less threat to cooperation.
6. Avoid excessive politeness or vague hedging if the situation calls for a clear, firm warning.

WORKED DEMONSTRATION — INDEPENDENT MODEL
In a fictional museum committee meeting, a member says, "I wouldn't dismiss the exhibition, but the labels need revision." This is not a promise that the exhibition is excellent, nor a request to cancel it. The speaker rejects an extreme conclusion while identifying one concrete defect. A faithful paraphrase is: "The idea remains worth considering, provided its labels are improved." The phrase "provided" makes the condition explicit.

COMMON PITFALL
Treating "could be clearer", "is unclear" and "is misleading" as interchangeable. The first often signals a gentle suggestion, the second describes a deficiency, and the third may imply the audience is led toward a false conclusion.

UNSCORED TRANSFER
Choose a short opinion you genuinely disagree with. Write two replies: one respectfully qualified and one deliberately direct. Explain which one better suits a school committee discussion. This exercise is self-reflection, not an automatic C2 writing score.
**Independent examples:**
1. Independent model: 'I would not dismiss the museum exhibition' rejects an extreme negative judgment without endorsing everything about the exhibition.
2. Meaning contrast: 'The labels could be clearer' is milder than 'The labels are misleading'; the two may lead to different practical responses.
3. Strategy: Preserve the speaker's attitude and degree of commitment before choosing a stylistically elegant synonym.

#### VOC-C2-01-Q1 · vocabulary.meaning_in_context · evidence_weight: 1
**Question:** A report says, 'I wouldn't go so far as to call the science-fair trial a failure.' Which paraphrase preserves its stance most accurately?
- A. The trial was successful in every meaningful respect.
- B. The trial may have fallen short, but 'failure' is too strong a conclusion.
- C. The trial was an unequivocal disaster.
- D. There is no basis on which anyone could evaluate the trial.
**Correct:** B. The trial may have fallen short, but 'failure' is too strong a conclusion.
**Why (post-submit):** The speaker resists the categorical label 'failure' without asserting success or denying that evaluation is possible.
**Next step:** Identify which extreme claim is being rejected, and avoid adding a positive verdict.

- D. The results are consistent with the proposal's promise but are not conclusive.
**Correct:** D. The results are consistent with the proposal's promise but are not conclusive.

**Next step:** Test whether each option turns limited evidence into certainty.

**Question:** An editorial states, 'The criticism of the new library app is not entirely unwarranted.' What does this most carefully communicate?
- A. Every complaint about the app has been proven.

**Correct:** D. At least some criticism has a reasonable basis, although it need not all be justified.
**Why (post-submit):** 'Not entirely unwarranted' uses qualified negation to recognise some legitimacy without endorsing every complaint.

#### VOC-C2-01-Q8 · vocabulary.meaning_in_context · evidence_weight: 1
**Question:** A club organiser writes, 'A private discussion would have been preferable to publicly correcting the new volunteer.' Which reading best captures the intended interpersonal effect?
- A. The public correction was certainly made with malicious intent.
- B. The organiser approves of public correction as the preferred approach.
- C. The organiser expresses a retrospective preference without directly accusing the person of bad motives.
- D. The organiser believes volunteers should never receive feedback.
**Correct:** C. The organiser expresses a retrospective preference without directly accusing the person of bad motives.
**Why (post-submit):** 'Would have been preferable' signals a counterfactual preference and tactful criticism of the approach, not an accusation about intention.
**Next step:** Distinguish an evaluation of an action from an unsupported claim about the actor's motives.
