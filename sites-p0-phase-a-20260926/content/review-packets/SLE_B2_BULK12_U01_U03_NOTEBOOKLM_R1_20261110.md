### B2-U01: GRA-B2-01 — How sure is the evidence? Present and past deduction

**Module:** grammar · **CEFR target:** B2 · **Estimated minutes:** 30 · **Proposed status:** UNREVIEWED

**CORE-1 — Goal and meaningful context**

By the end, distinguish evidence-based certainty (`must`, `might/could`, `cannot`) from instructions, and select present or perfect infinitive forms when referring to now versus earlier events. The aim is to calibrate a claim, not merely insert a modal.

**Why this matters:** Claims about school records, online messages and project decisions can sound confident without being supported.

**CORE-2 — Explicit strategy and INDEPENDENT worked demonstration**

**Step 1 — identify the proposition and its time.** `must be` / `cannot be` interpret a present state; `must have + past participle` / `might have + past participle` interpret an earlier event. **Step 2 — inspect evidence:** direct and trustworthy records can justify stronger deduction; a suggestive clue usually permits several explanations. **Step 3 — choose strength:** `must` = strong reasonable conclusion, `might/could` = possibility, `cannot` = ruled out by evidence. **Step 4 — separate obligation:** in `You must wear a badge`, the word expresses a rule, not evidence-based confidence. `Mustn't` normally expresses prohibition, not 'impossible in the past'.

**Independent worked model (not used in any graded stem or source):**

A community garden gate was photographed securely closed at 18:00, and a verified staff record shows it was locked at 17:45. A reasonable past deduction is: “The caretaker must have locked it before the photo.” Without that record, the photograph alone would support “Someone might have locked it earlier”; it does not identify who did it. The model explains the evidence, the timing and the alternative.

**Typical pitfall and contrast:**

“Nobody answered the invitation, so they can't have read it” incorrectly turns absence of a reply into proof of non-reading. “They might have read it without replying” is more defensible.

### B2-U01: GRA-B2-02 — If things had happened differently: counterfactual meaning

**Module:** grammar · **CEFR target:** B2 · **Estimated minutes:** 32 · **Proposed status:** UNREVIEWED

**CORE-1 — Goal and meaningful context**

Interpret second, third and mixed conditional meanings; distinguish a proposed future change from an imaginary past event, and use qualifications instead of presenting hypothetical outcomes as documented facts.

**Why this matters:** Planning improvements means discussing what might change without falsely claiming the outcome already happened.

**CORE-2 — Explicit strategy and INDEPENDENT worked demonstration**

**Future-real option:** `If + present, will/can + verb` concerns a possible later outcome, not a certainty. **Imaginary present:** `If + past form, would/could + verb` imagines a present situation that differs from reality. **Past counterfactual:** `If + past perfect, would/could have + past participle` imagines an earlier unreal condition and its earlier outcome. **Mixed past→present:** `If + past perfect, would/could + verb now` links an unreal earlier condition to an imagined present consequence. In scientific or school recommendations, a grammatical hypothetical is NOT evidence that the consequence would definitely occur; use might/could where uncertainty matters.

**Independent worked model (not used in any graded stem or source):**

A youth orchestra had no rehearsal room last term and currently lacks a recorded concert. An honest counterfactual is “If the group had secured a room last term, it might have recorded a concert.” The form expresses a possible unreal earlier outcome, not proof that a recording was inevitable. “If it secures a room next term, it could plan a recording” expresses a real future option.

**Typical pitfall and contrast:**

“If we collected more data yesterday, we will prove the idea” mixes an unreal past situation with an unjustified certainty. Decide the time of each clause before choosing a form; a conditional does not validate causation.

**CORE-3 — Independent graded practice**

Choose exactly **one best option** for each item. Do not show the reviewer-only key, rationale or distractor notes until Submit. Every objective item has `evidence_weight: 1`; these are practice scores, not CEFR certification.

### B2-U01: REA-B2-02 — Read cautious proposals: what has been decided?

**Module:** reading · **CEFR target:** B2 · **Estimated minutes:** 32 · **Proposed status:** UNREVIEWED

**CORE-1 — Goal and meaningful context**

Identify conditional decisions, distinguish a measured finding from a prediction, and compare how two sources qualify a proposed school-community service change.

**Why this matters:** Well-written proposals often distinguish evidence already gathered from benefits that still need testing.

**CORE-2 — Explicit strategy and INDEPENDENT worked demonstration**

**Read in four passes:** (1) locate what has already happened; (2) identify modal verbs and if-clauses marking a possible future; (3) find sample limits or missing measurements; (4) compare recommendations and who might be excluded. A qualified recommendation such as “test for six weeks” is neither a rejection nor a confirmed permanent change. Distinguish response percentages among participants from claims about a whole community.

**Independent worked model (not used in any graded stem or source):**

Independent model: A sports centre report says 38 of 50 survey respondents liked a pilot, but the survey was offered only to people attending the pilot. The cautious conclusion is that most *respondents* were positive, not that most neighbourhood residents would favour it. This model uses a different location, population and policy from the assessed sources.

**Typical pitfall and contrast:**

“Most poll respondents preferred it” does not mean “most eligible users preferred it”; also, might extend is not an announcement that opening hours have already changed.

**CORE-3 — Independent graded practice**

### B2-U01: WRI-B2-01 — Recommend a conditional library trial without overclaiming

**Module:** writing · **CEFR target:** B2 · **Estimated minutes:** 35 · **Proposed status:** UNREVIEWED

**CORE-1 — Goal and meaningful context**

Write a balanced advisory email that distinguishes a proposal from an approved plan and uses appropriate conditional language to recommend an evidence-gathering next step.

**CORE-2 — Explicit composing strategy**

**Plan:** (1) state the decision still open; (2) cite one fact from each source; (3) distinguish reliable observation from uncertain interpretation; (4) propose a feasible, reversible trial with named measurements; (5) explain one access or staffing safeguard; (6) revise modal strength and register before submission. Use “may/could/might” for forecast benefits and clear conditionals for recommendations.

**Independent worked demonstration (unrelated task; NOT a model answer for assessed prompt):**

Independent miniature example (different topic): A youth museum considers moving its guided tour to Friday. A short outline would acknowledge the current visitor counts, explain uncertainty about families who attend on Saturdays, and propose a four-week trial with anonymous attendance counts. This is an outline of *reasoning steps*, not a prepared answer about the library.

**Common pitfall and revision contrast:** “The poll proves all students need late opening” misstates the population; revise to “The poll suggests a preference among respondents, but other students were not reached.”

### B2-U03: VOC-B2-04 — Words that clarify the structure of an argument

**Module:** vocabulary · **CEFR target:** B2 · **Estimated minutes:** 29 · **Proposed status:** UNREVIEWED

**CORE-1 — Goal and meaningful context**

Use argument-analysis vocabulary precisely: premise, qualification, counterclaim, implication, limitation, and conclusion; choose natural related word forms in context without confusing a concern with a refutation.

**Why this matters:** A reader needs to know whether a sentence supplies evidence, limits a claim, challenges it or asks for a decision.

**CORE-2 — Explicit strategy and INDEPENDENT worked demonstration**

**Premise** = a statement used to support reasoning; it may be true or false and is not automatically evidence. **Qualification** = a limit or condition on the strength/scope of a claim. **Counterclaim** = a different position; it must be evaluated rather than simply dismissed. **Implication** = what may follow if the claim is accepted; it is not necessarily an observed outcome. **Limitation** = a weakness in the scope, measurement or applicability of evidence. **Refute** is stronger than question: a genuine counterexample can refute a universal claim, but merely raising a concern does not. A **concession** acknowledges something reasonable in a different view without conceding the entire argument.

**Independent worked model (not used in any graded stem or source):**

Independent model: A town website says “Every evening bus arrives on time.” One verified late arrival is a counterexample that refutes the universal word every. A note saying some passengers dislike the timetable raises a separate concern but does not logically refute the punctuality claim. The model concerns transport, not the school-community sources in these assessed items.

**Typical pitfall and contrast:**

“I have a counterclaim, therefore your data are false” is a non sequitur. A counterclaim may introduce a different explanation without disproving the original observation.
