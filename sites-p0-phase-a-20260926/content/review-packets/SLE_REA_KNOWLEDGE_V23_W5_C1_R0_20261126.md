## Appendix A — Proposed learner-visible Knowledge

### REA-C1-01 — Interpret a qualified stance beneath the surface
**Goal EN:** Infer the writer's qualified position across a demanding commentary; distinguish observation, sampling limits, implication and recommendation; explain how concession, understatement, evaluative wording and figurative language shape that position without attributing unsupported intentions.
**Goal VI:** Suy ra lập trường có điều kiện xuyên suốt bài bình luận nâng cao; phân biệt quan sát, giới hạn mẫu, hàm ý và khuyến nghị; giải thích cách nhượng bộ, nói giảm, từ ngữ đánh giá và hình ảnh ẩn dụ định hình lập trường mà không gán ý định thiếu căn cứ.
**Context EN:** An accomplished reader distinguishes a writer's judgment of an idea from their judgment of a particular claim made on its behalf. Tone and implication can narrow a position without reversing it; the text must warrant the inference.
**Context VI:** Người đọc thành thạo phân biệt đánh giá của tác giả về một ý tưởng với đánh giá về một khẳng định cụ thể dùng để ủng hộ ý tưởng đó. Giọng điệu và hàm ý có thể thu hẹp lập trường mà không đảo ngược nó; suy luận phải có căn cứ văn bản.
#### Strategy cards
- Track the argument across paragraphs / Theo dõi lập luận qua các đoạn: Mark the proposition under debate, initial concession, subsequent reservations and final recommendation; interpret individual criticisms within that progression. / Đánh dấu đề xuất đang bàn, nhượng bộ ban đầu, các dè dặt tiếp theo và khuyến nghị cuối; hiểu từng phê bình trong tiến trình đó.
- Evidence and reach / Bằng chứng và phạm vi: Distinguish counts, reported experiences and generalisations; identify recruitment limits and the gap between an indicator and the intended outcome. Ask whether an apparent preference could be shaped by the people who were reached. / Phân biệt số đếm, trải nghiệm báo cáo và khái quát; xác định giới hạn tuyển mẫu và khoảng cách giữa chỉ báo với kết quả mong muốn. Hỏi liệu sở thích ghi nhận có thể chịu ảnh hưởng của nhóm được tiếp cận.
- Rhetorical force / Sức nặng tu từ: Explain what understatement, contrast, concession or figurative comparison invites readers to infer. Cite the exact wording and nearby context; distinguish a pointed criticism of reasoning from an accusation of dishonesty or a rejection of the underlying proposal. / Giải thích điều nói giảm, tương phản, nhượng bộ hoặc so sánh hình tượng khiến người đọc suy ra. Dẫn đúng từ ngữ và bối cảnh gần; phân biệt phê bình sắc bén về lập luận với cáo buộc gian dối hay bác bỏ đề xuất gốc.
- Qualified synthesis / Tổng hợp có điều kiện: Retain the strongest justified observation, identify concrete competing interests or limits, and connect the recommendation to those limits. Avoid both categorical overstatement and vague neutrality. / Giữ quan sát có căn cứ mạnh nhất, xác định lợi ích cạnh tranh hoặc giới hạn cụ thể, nối khuyến nghị với giới hạn đó. Tránh cả khẳng định tuyệt đối lẫn trung lập mơ hồ.
#### Interactive worked text — independent model: An audio description service at a botanical exhibition
S1. When a botanical exhibition introduced short audio descriptions beside selected plant displays, the proposal was praised for offering visitors another way to engage with the collection. S2. Extending the recordings to every display might eventually be a sensible investment; the case for doing so is, for the moment, less settled than the promotional language implies.

S3. The equipment log recorded 310 activations over four weeks, and 76 activations included replaying at least one description. S4. Such records are useful indicators of engagement with the equipment, although they count activations rather than distinct listeners and tell us nothing about how well visitors understood the displays. S5. In a separate voluntary feedback form distributed to visitors who had used a recording, 39 of 47 respondents described it as helpful. S6. The commendation is worth taking seriously, but the route by which the opinions were collected makes any claim about visitors in general considerably more ambitious than the evidence.

S7. Nor does agreement on the desirability of an additional format amount to agreement on its design: some listeners valued rich botanical detail, whereas others found lengthy explanations difficult to follow while moving between displays. S8. It would be a curious definition of improvement to make each recording longer simply because one group asked for greater depth. S9. The issue is not whether either preference deserves respect, but whether the format can accommodate both without imposing one group's needs on the other.

S10. The exhibition should therefore investigate a short description with an optional extended explanation, seek feedback through routes that also reach nonusers, and assess comprehension as well as equipment use. S11. A tally is an instrument for inquiry, not a verdict engraved in stone: the present records justify examining the service's potential, not declaring its design complete. S12. The proposed expansion deserves consideration, provided the next evaluation establishes how different visitors actually use and understand the descriptions and how much staff time maintaining them requires.

The first three months produced 96 recorded box loans, and 30 of 36 people completing a voluntary return-form questionnaire described the guides as useful; no forms were sent to groups that had not borrowed a box. T3. To call the guides redundant would be difficult to reconcile with those responses; to describe them as adequate for every group would be equally hasty. T4. Some borrowers appreciated concise planting calendars, while others wanted more detail on growing conditions, and the seed bank has not assessed germination outcomes. T5. The coordinator recommends trialling a concise core guide with optional detailed sheets, approaching groups outside the borrower list and recording outcomes where participants voluntarily agree. T6. The published note concludes that the guide is a promising starting point, not a finished blueprint for every garden.

### REA-C1-01 — Read between the lines: stance, evidence and qualified support
Objectives: Identify a writer's qualified position in a demanding school-policy commentary without treating criticism as outright rejection. / Distinguish observed figures, survey limitations, implied concerns and recommendations, citing precise wording. / Explain how concessive and evaluative wording guides readers toward a cautious interpretation.
Passage PASS-REA-C1-01 — Why the lunchtime studio should remain an experiment:
WHY THE LUNCHTIME STUDIO SHOULD REMAIN AN EXPERIMENT

When Eastbridge School converted a spare classroom into a lunchtime studio, the announcement promised a place where pupils could sketch, build prototypes or simply develop an idea without being graded. Its most enthusiastic supporters called it "a room for everyone". Six weeks later, the school committee is considering making the arrangement permanent. That is a reasonable possibility, but a premature conclusion.

The early figures are encouraging without being decisive. Forty-six pupils visited at least once; nineteen returned on three or more occasions. Those figures tell us that the room attracted interest. They do not tell us whether interest was widespread, whether regular visitors benefited more than occasional visitors, or whether pupils who stayed away had been offered a realistic opportunity to come.

A questionnaire completed by twenty-eight visitors was similarly positive: twenty-four described the studio as useful. It would be perverse to dismiss their experience. Yet the questionnaire, distributed inside the room, could scarcely reveal the views of pupils who never entered it. One need not be an expert in statistics to notice that omission. The headline "Most pupils support the studio", which appeared in an early newsletter draft, therefore goes further than the evidence allows.

Nor is the room's purpose as straightforward as its publicity suggests. Some visitors say that the chance to talk through an idea is its main advantage. Others appreciate somewhere quiet enough to concentrate. Both groups have a defensible claim on the space, but a single room cannot satisfy them simultaneously without some thought about how it is used. A notice asking everyone to "work creatively" does little to settle the issue.

None of this amounts to an argument for closing the studio. Equally, keeping the door unlocked is not quite the same as making the opportunity accessible. Pupils with lunchtime responsibilities or activities may never be free during the studio's advertised hours. The committee could try two quieter sessions each week, allow a limited number of supervised visits at another time, and invite feedback from pupils who have not attended. It could then examine not merely how many visits take place but whose participation changes.

Enthusiasm is worth preserving; it is not a substitute for inquiry. The choice is not between celebrating the project and obstructing it. It is between treating the pilot as a promising idea that can still be improved and treating applause as if it were the evidence that no improvement is needed.
