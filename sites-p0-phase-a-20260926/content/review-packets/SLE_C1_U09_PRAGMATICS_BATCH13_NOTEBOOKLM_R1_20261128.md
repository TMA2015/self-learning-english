## 1. REA-C1-10 — Interpret indirect meaning across turns
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Infer what a participant is doing with a written message (request, tentative objection, reservation, invitation to revise or closure) while keeping competing plausible interpretations open until later turns provide evidence. Distinguish a supported interpretation of an utterance from a claim about the writer's private motives.
**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** Literal proposition is what the words explicitly say; pragmatic force is the action the words perform in context. An indirect question may invite clarification or signal a difficulty; politeness may soften disagreement without changing its substance. Use a turn-by-turn map: prior proposal → literal words → likely action and its evidence → later uptake or clarification → remaining uncertainty. An inference can be cancelled or revised: “I was wondering whether…” does not always mean the same thing. Do not infer intention from a single hedge, emoji, response delay or supposed national convention. A direct statement may be appropriate when a deadline or safety boundary matters; a highly mitigated message may be unhelpfully ambiguous. Independent worked model: a community choir's scheduler writes, “Would the smaller room still work?” after a colleague mentions a new piano. This could ask about space, or indirectly flag suitability. The reply “The issue is access for the piano, not audience size” clarifies the concern; a further reply “Then let's check the doorway before deciding” shows uptake. Before that clarification, neither concern nor motive was certain. A repair question can name the two readings without accusing anyone.
**CORE-3 — Independent reading source (fictional).**
Written thread from a volunteer exhibition team planning a printed guide. The team has agreed to discuss a draft but not authorised printing. A design partner works asynchronously with the team.

Monday, team channel — Alex: “I've uploaded the guide. Unless there are major objections, I suggest sending it to the printer on Thursday. We can settle the caption wording later.”
Monday, partner channel reply — Sam: “Thursday would be rather ambitious from our end. I wonder whether the caption review belongs after printing.”
Monday, Alex: “Thanks. We could move the printing to Friday if Thursday is inconvenient.”
Tuesday, Sam: “Sorry, I wasn't referring primarily to the day. Some captions describe loans from other organisations, and those descriptions need their confirmation before the print file is released. Friday would make no difference if the confirmations were still outstanding.”
Tuesday, Alex: “Understood. I had taken your first message as a scheduling concern. Shall we separate the date from the sign-off? I can prepare a print-ready draft, but not send it until the confirmations arrive.”
Tuesday, Jo (coordinator): “That would be helpful. For clarity, the Thursday meeting is a review, not sign-off. A printer slot can be requested provisionally; nobody should commit to it. If confirmations do not arrive, we can circulate the draft internally with the captions marked pending.”
Wednesday, Sam (private message to Alex): “I appreciate the distinction. My earlier wording was intended to raise the order of the steps, not to suggest the design work was behind schedule. I'd prefer any unresolved wording to be discussed in the shared channel so the relevant people can respond.”
Wednesday, Alex (shared channel): “To confirm our next steps: I'll prepare the draft; loan partners will be asked to confirm the relevant captions; Jo will review status on Thursday. The release date remains undecided. Please flag anything I've misunderstood.”

## 2. REA-C1-11 — Negotiate different communication conventions without stereotyping
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Recognise how different locally learned conventions, role expectations and communication channels can create misunderstanding; choose a repair that acknowledges both interpretations, establishes an explicit shared convention and protects actual decision authority.
**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** In a distributed team, “noted”, “approved”, a silence or a reaction icon can have different operational meanings. Do not assume one convention is universal or explain differences by nationality. Look for participants' stated practices, task risk, hierarchy, time zone or channel constraints; separate the impact of a misunderstanding from an accusation of intent. A productive repair often has five moves: describe the observable mismatch; recognise the other interpretation without endorsing an incorrect conclusion; state the essential boundary plainly; ask or offer a precise way forward; confirm the shared meaning and who can act. Acknowledgement does not mean concession of authority. When decisions are consequential, explicit wording may be more considerate than excessive indirectness. Independent worked model: in a fictional neighbourhood event team, one volunteer uses a check-mark reaction to mean “read”, while a supplier's coordinator treats it as “ready to order”. A repair could be “I see why the check mark looked like permission. Here it meant receipt only. Before ordering, please wait for the message ‘Order approved’ from the event lead. Would that wording work for both teams?” This recognises the mismatch, names the limit and proposes a shared convention; it does not infer dishonesty or national habits.
**CORE-3 — Independent written exchange (fictional).**
A community language exchange network is preparing a joint programme with a partner centre. Two groups have different established procedures; people work in several locations. No nationality is specified.

Partner coordinator, Noor: “Our team can put the provisional session list on the public page today if there is no objection by the afternoon. In our usual planning threads, silence after the review window is treated as acceptance for routine publicity.”
Network coordinator, Eli: “Please hold off. I had read the draft as being circulated for comments. Our network requires the venue lead to confirm room capacity and accessibility before any public schedule appears. Several colleagues have not read the thread because they are working different shifts.”
Noor: “I understand the capacity check. From our side, keeping the page unpublished creates a different problem: people have already asked whether registration will open soon. I don't want the partner centre to appear unresponsive. Could we at least show that a programme is being prepared?”
Venue lead, Robin: “I can confirm one room for the introductory session. I cannot yet confirm the other rooms or their access arrangements. I would be comfortable with an update that gives no times, venue details or registration link, provided it says the schedule is awaiting confirmation.”
Eli: “That seems workable in principle. To avoid a repeat, can we label each shared item either ‘for comment’ or ‘approved for publication’, with the person responsible for approval named? No response would mean no recorded approval in these joint threads.”
Noor: “That would work for me if the approval request includes a clear reply-by time and a contact for questions. When we miss a reply, we could ask the responsible person rather than assume a decision. I'll draft a short holding update for Robin's check, not publish it yet.”
Robin: “Agreed on that process. I will check the draft holding update. I am not approving the full programme or registration today.”

REA-C1-11-Q1 — What best explains the original misunderstanding on the available evidence?
- A. Noor wants to avoid the venue lead's responsibility and Eli objects to publicity altogether. — Neither motive is established.
- B. Both teams already treat silence as explicit approval but disagree about accessibility. — Their stated conventions differ.
- C. Noor assumes a familiar local silence-as-acceptance practice applies; Eli sees circulation as consultation and has a separate explicit approval requirement. — **Key C.** Identifies the procedural difference without stereotyping.
- D. The disagreement is solely about which colleagues can read messages on a given shift. — Shift access matters but does not explain the distinct approval rules alone.

REA-C1-11-Q2 — Which interpretation of Noor's second message best preserves its interpersonal and practical force?
- A. Noor accepts the capacity concern while proposing a narrower way to address audience uncertainty and responsiveness. — **Key A.** Concedes the constraint, then seeks a distinct limited action.
- B. Noor withdraws the original request and agrees to give no public information until every room is confirmed. — A holding update is explicitly proposed.
- C. Noor argues that audience expectations should override accessibility checks. — Noor acknowledges the check and asks for narrower information.
- D. Noor's concern proves the public already expects a confirmed programme and registration date. — Enquiries do not establish those expectations.

REA-C1-11-Q3 — Which holding update would meet Robin's stated boundaries?
- A. “The introductory session has a confirmed room; other rooms will be announced next week. Registration is coming shortly.” — Adds timing and programme specifics beyond the authorised draft boundary.
- B. “The programme is being prepared; the schedule is awaiting confirmation. Further information will follow once arrangements are confirmed.” — **Key B.** No times, venue details or registration link, and uncertainty is explicit.
- C. “All venues have passed preliminary access checks; a public schedule will follow.” — Invents checks.
- D. “The programme is delayed because the venue team has not responded.” — Adds blame and an unsupported delay claim.

REA-C1-11-Q4 — Which description of Eli's proposed convention is most accurate?
- A. It makes silence an approval signal once the reply-by time is stated. — Silence remains non-approval.
- B. It prevents all colleagues from commenting on items already under review. — No such exclusion.
- C. It gives all team members equal authority to approve publication. — A responsible approver is to be named.
- D. It makes item status and authority explicit, replacing inferred approval from silence with a recorded approval requirement. — **Key D.** Reflects the operational change.

REA-C1-11-Q5 — Why is Noor's reply-by condition a constructive addition rather than a contradiction of Eli's proposal?
- A. It restores automatic approval when the reply window closes. — The follow-up is to ask the approver, not assume.
- B. It changes the venue lead's role to a passive observer. — Named responsibility is retained.
- C. It makes the explicit process more predictable and supplies a way to follow up without treating non-response as consent. — **Key C.** Responds to coordination needs while respecting the boundary.
- D. It establishes that everyone will read the message before the deadline. — A deadline cannot guarantee reading.

REA-C1-11-Q6 — Which account of the final position is supported?
- A. The team agrees a process; the holding update is to be drafted and checked, but the full programme and registration are not approved. — **Key A.** Distinguishes process agreement from publication decisions.
- B. Robin has approved the holding update itself because Robin agreed to check it. — A future check is not approval.
- C. The introductory room confirmation authorises publication of the entire programme. — Approval scope is explicitly limited.
- D. The original silence-as-acceptance convention has been abolished in all partner-centre work. — The new convention concerns joint threads only.

## 3. WRI-C1-06 — Repair disagreement and negotiate a workable next step in writing
**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Compose a constructive written response to a multi-party disagreement involving implicit criticism, different expectations and an authority boundary. Represent each position fairly, clarify uncertainty without assigning motive, propose a workable shared process, and revise for both relational tone and practical clarity.
**CORE-2 — Teach/model.** Parse Topic (the mismatch), Task (reply and follow-up), Must Include (recognise both concerns, precise clarification, boundary, process, ownership), Boundary (no assumed motives, national stereotypes, promises or unauthorised actions). Brainstorm an interpretation map before writing: what was said, possible intended action, observed impact, clarification needed, shared goal and remaining conflict. Select wording that is tactful but not evasive. Separate acknowledgement (“I see why the note read that way”) from acceptance of a claim (“that does not mean the plan was authorised”). Offer a neutral rephrasing, one focused question and a conditional next action, rather than demanding that one party apologise or pretending all concerns are resolved. Draft, then test each sentence: would the addressee recognise their position, can an uninvolved reader identify what is agreed, and could anyone mistake a request for permission? Independent annotated model, different prompt: a fictional student art group sends a draft invitation labelled “final layout”; the venue contact understands that as a request for event approval, while the designer means layout work is finished. “I can see why ‘final’ suggested permission to proceed [acknowledge impact]. Here it described the layout only [clarify scope]. Could we check whether your review concerns the venue or the wording [focused question]? I'll relabel the document ‘layout ready; event approval pending’ and wait for the venue contact's confirmation before sending it [concrete action and boundary].” The designer's intention is not inferred from nationality or personality; the label mismatch is addressed.
