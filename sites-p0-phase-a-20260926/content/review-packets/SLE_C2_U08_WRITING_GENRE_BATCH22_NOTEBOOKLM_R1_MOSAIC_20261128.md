## 1. WRI-C2-03 — Develop and revise a sustained critical essay

**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Write a nuanced critical essay for an educated general reader that uses explicit evaluative criteria, integrates close analysis of wording and structure, responds to a plausible alternative reading and revises the governing thesis as evidence warrants. Do not simply summarise the material or advocate an unqualified policy. Target C2; 60–80 minutes advisory, can split into analysis/first draft and revision.

**CORE-2 — Teach/model.**

### Step 1: Analyse the task and brainstorm
Topic = what the two texts ask a reader to notice; Task = critical evaluation, not a report of events; Must Include = a thesis, criteria, concrete textual evidence, alternative interpretation, qualified synthesis; Boundary = source-supported analysis, no claims about private intentions or observed audience effects unless evidence establishes them.
List two or three criteria that matter to the intended reader, such as interpretive openness, accountability for claims, and coherence between a text's opening and ending. For each criterion collect specific phrases and a possible alternative reading. Ask what each source makes visible and what it leaves unresolved. Avoid treating complexity as automatically superior to simplicity.

### Step 2: Outline and draft
Plan a genuine progression rather than one paragraph per source:
- Opening: frame the evaluative tension and a provisional thesis that can survive counterevidence.
- Body: introduce a criterion, analyse the language (not merely quote it), connect the analysis to the thesis; compare how another text handles the same issue. Use a second criterion that could complicate the first.
- Counter-reading: state the strongest plausible alternative fairly; show what it explains and what it cannot establish.
- Ending: return to a more precise version of the governing thesis; make clear the limits of the evaluation, not a mechanical recap.
Use critical verbs with different force: "foregrounds", "invites", "allows", "presupposes", "asserts", "establishes". A text may invite an interpretation without proving that actual readers adopted it. Use transitions that mark a shift of criterion, not ornamental connectors.

### Step 3: Independent worked demonstration (different topic)
Fictional two captions for a restored clock:
X: "At last the clock tells the town's story again. Its hands have been returned to their rightful place."
Y: "The new hands follow a drawing dated 1890. The dial retains marks from later alterations. The restoration chooses one documented arrangement without claiming to recover the clock's only authentic state."
Draft thesis: "Y is accurate, whereas X is dishonest." Problem: the texts support a contrast in evidential transparency, but neither establishes dishonesty; X can offer an appealing sense of continuity.
Annotated model paragraph:
"Both captions seek to connect a physical object with a shared past, but they distribute interpretive responsibility differently. X's 'at last' makes the restoration sound like the completion of an overdue task, while 'rightful' presents one arrangement as if it were self-evidently legitimate. The wording is economical and might give a visitor an immediate reason to care; its limitation is that the basis of the choice is not stated. Y, by contrast, anchors the choice in a dated drawing and acknowledges subsequent alterations. Its greater precision does not make it an exhaustive history of the clock. It does, however, enable a reader to distinguish the documented arrangement from the broader claim of authenticity. On the criterion of transparent historical interpretation, that distinction is more consequential than the captions' difference in emotional appeal."
Why this works: sentence 1 provides the comparison criterion; the quotation analysis distinguishes implied legitimacy from a factual claim; the concession preserves X's plausible strength; the final sentence weighs the criterion rather than declaring a universal winner.
Revised thesis: "X offers an economical narrative of continuity; Y makes the evidential basis of restoration more recoverable, a particular advantage when the purpose is to invite scrutiny of historical choices." The revision narrows the judgment to a purpose, without weakening it into empty balance.

### Step 4: Revise a whole text
Reverse-outline each paragraph in one sentence. If two paragraphs perform the same argumentative job, merge or differentiate them; if a late counter-reading changes the thesis, revise the introduction as well as the ending. Audit quotations, modal force, precision of reference and rhythm. Use a short sentence deliberately for a decisive contrast, not as a mechanical rule. Keep V1 and V2 or separate labelled copies. An edit counts as substantive when it changes the argument's structure, claim boundary or explanatory adequacy, not just its spelling.

### SRC-WRI-C2-03 — Mosaic exhibition labels and interpretive responsibility

A museum is developing an exhibition of an incomplete floor mosaic. The exhibition's stated purpose is to encourage visitors to understand both what remains and how interpretations are developed, without requiring prior specialist knowledge. The following are draft texts, not final approved labels.

A — Proposed opening label:
"Step into the courtyard of a vanished household. At its centre, a bird spreads its wings towards a fountain, an emblem of a home that once welcomed strangers. Time has taken part of the floor, but the reconstructed border restores the scene's coherence. Follow the pattern and you will discover the story its makers intended."

B — Proposed alternative opening label:
"The surviving central fragments show part of a bird and a curved feature that has been interpreted as a fountain. A reconstruction of the outer border uses repeated motifs from two surviving corners. The proposed arrangement is consistent with those fragments, although another arrangement would also fit. We do not know who commissioned the mosaic or what its makers intended the bird to signify. The reconstruction makes one possible scene easier to examine; it is not a record of the entire original floor."

C — Curatorial working note: the bird and curved feature are visible in surviving fragments. A fountain is plausible, not confirmed; the link to hospitality is one proposed symbolic reading. The two corners support a repeating border, but the lost stretches cannot be reconstructed uniquely. An outline of the missing area will be visible in the display. Neither label has been visitor-tested. A colleague argues that beginning with a vivid invitation may be more engaging than opening with qualifications. Another argues that the visitor should be able to identify interpretive choices without first mistaking them for documented facts. Neither colleague rejects imagination or evidence; they differ over sequencing and emphasis. No final wording has been approved.

WRI-C2-03-Q1 — Write a 340–450-word critical essay for the museum's public education journal. Develop a defensible thesis using at least two named or clearly articulated evaluative criteria; analyse precise phrases from both drafts; examine the strongest plausible alternative to your initial judgment; explain the significance of the visible outline of the missing area and what cannot be inferred from the absence of visitor testing. You may favour A, B, or a qualified combination for a stated purpose. Do not invent a definitive reconstruction, historical symbolism, audience reaction or approval. A recommendation is optional; the principal genre is a critical essay, not an action plan.

Produce V1, then V2 after reverse-outlining. Add a 90–130-word revision commentary: quote one changed thesis or paragraph-level claim; explain one structural change and one lexical/modality change; identify one attractive overstatement you rejected. If the first thesis survives, justify why and show another substantive argument-level repair. Keep quotations short and faithful. No single acceptable model essay.

## 2. WRI-C2-04 — Turn critical reflection into a precise professional proposal

**CORE-1 — Goal/context.** Write a coherent first-person critical reflection grounded in a supplied fictional experience, distinguish observation from retrospective interpretation, examine how an initial assumption changes and identify the limits of a new understanding. Recast the insight as a concise professional proposal whose evidence, scope, audience and action differ appropriately from the reflective genre. Revise both without inventing experiences, motives, causal effects or approval. Target C2; 65–85 minutes advisory, split visits allowed.

**CORE-2 — Teach/model.**

### Step 1: Analyse and brainstorm
Topic = a recorded experience and its meaning; Task = reflective analysis plus a professional proposal; Must Include = initial interpretation, turning point, reconsideration, unresolved question, evidence-based action; Boundary = write as the fictional narrator using only supplied experiences. First-person does not license invented feelings or autobiography. Separate (a) what happened, (b) what the narrator thought at the time, (c) what the narrator can reasonably infer now, (d) what remains unknown, (e) a possible next step. Avoid the simplistic arc of mistake → complete certainty.

### Step 2: Outline two different genre structures
Reflection: select a concrete episode → explain the initial assumption → examine the detail that challenges it → consider alternative explanations → reformulate understanding → identify a question for future experience. Temporal sequencing supports analysis; it should not become an exhaustive diary. A first-person admission can be an accountable explanation rather than a confession.
Proposal: state the decision sought → specify relevant observations and their limits → compare a feasible adjustment against the existing arrangement → state responsibilities, dependencies and a review condition. The writer's personal turning point belongs only insofar as it explains a relevant uncertainty; the reader should not have to accept the narrator's introspection as evidence. Use explicit ownership rather than indiscriminate passive voice.

### Step 3: Independent worked demonstration (different topic)
A fictional language-club organiser records that two participants repeatedly asked about the order of activities. The organiser initially assumed they had not read the schedule. Later, one participant points to two activities printed under the same heading; the organiser discovers the schedule did not distinguish a discussion from a preparation period. No record establishes why the second participant asked. The committee may approve a revised schedule; the organiser can draft it now.
Reflective draft: "I realised that the participants had ignored the schedule because it was confusing." This simultaneously imputes behaviour and claims a causal explanation for both participants.
Revised reflective passage:
"I had taken repeated questions as a sign that the schedule had gone unread. When one participant pointed to the shared heading, I recognised a distinction I had assumed the document already made. I could now identify a weakness in my own explanation of the sequence, but not establish why both participants had asked. The episode changed the question I would put to a reader: instead of asking whether the schedule had been read, I would ask whether it made the two purposes distinguishable."
Professional proposal passage:
"Separate the discussion and preparation periods under distinct headings in the next draft. One participant identified the shared heading as a source of difficulty, and the current schedule does not distinguish the two purposes. The organiser can prepare a revision for committee review; its effect on readers' understanding should be checked before wider use."
Annotation: the reflective version foregrounds changed interpretation and retains uncertainty about the second participant. The proposal foregrounds a specific, bounded action and its evidence, omits irrelevant private chronology, and preserves authority. The proposal does not turn a single comment into a general effectiveness finding. Neither genre is necessarily more formal or more truthful than the other.

### Step 4: Draft and revise with genre-sensitive checks
Draft reflection first; mark where a concrete incident becomes an interpretation. Draft the proposal from a separate evidence/action ledger, not by replacing "I" with "the team". Reverse-outline both: remove repeated narrative detail that does not develop the reflection, and add material operational limits missing from the proposal. Compare wording of claims in both versions. A change of register may alter prominence and tone, never certainty or authority. Choose one subtle lexical edit (e.g., "recognised a possible barrier" versus "proved the cause") and explain why. Preserve both drafts before revising.

### SRC-WRI-C2-04 — Chamber music rehearsal journal

A community chamber music ensemble is planning a series of open rehearsals for learners. Write as fictional rehearsal assistant Minh, using the supplied journal extracts and notes; do not invent other personal experiences.

A — Journal, before rehearsal: "I assumed that a clear printed sequence of pieces would allow visitors to follow our decisions. I thought spoken explanations between pieces would interrupt the rehearsal, so I proposed limiting them to the opening and closing remarks."

B — Journal, first open rehearsal: twelve visitors attended. During a repeated passage, the players stopped, discussed an entry and began again. Two visitors separately asked whether the repetition meant the performance had gone wrong. I pointed to the piece number on the printed sequence, which identified the piece but not the purpose of the repetition. One visitor said, "I knew where you were in the programme; I couldn't tell what you were trying to change." I noticed that my answer had addressed location rather than purpose. I do not know what the other ten visitors understood. I did not record whether extra explanations affected rehearsal time.

C — Conductor's note: pauses serve different purposes: practising an entry, comparing a dynamic balance, or discussing an interpretation. Sometimes a brief cue can make the purpose legible; in other moments an explanation could interrupt concentration. The conductor supports considering brief cues at selected transitions, not commentary at every pause. The printed sequence remains useful for identifying pieces. No educational gain or effect on rehearsal flow has been measured.

D — Planning constraint: the assistant can draft a cue sheet with three candidate transition points and prepare a short optional anonymous question about the purpose of a repetition. Only the conductor can approve which cues are used; the ensemble committee must approve a further public rehearsal. The next closed rehearsal has ten minutes available for testing the wording and timing of possible cues. No further public session is approved.

WRI-C2-04-Q1 — Produce two related but distinct texts.
(a) A 260–350-word critical reflection as Minh for the ensemble's learning journal. Develop the initial assumption, explain the turning point in the visitor's comment, consider a plausible alternative to the conclusion that more explanations are always needed, and explain how your revised understanding distinguishes following the sequence from understanding the purpose of a rehearsal decision. Preserve the limits of the observation and the conductor's concern. Use a reflective voice without inventing feelings, additional dialogue or visitor reactions.
(b) A 170–230-word professional proposal for the conductor and committee. Request a bounded closed-rehearsal test or justify a different feasible next step. Explain the rationale, retain the useful role of the printed sequence, account for the ten-minute constraint, specify responsibility and an evaluation question, and distinguish the closed test from a possible future public event. Do not assert measured gains, timing effects or public approval.

Keep V1 and V2 for both texts. Add a 90–130-word revision commentary: explain two changes of genre function (not merely formality), one change to preserve uncertainty, and one substantive change to organisation or reasoning. Quote the actual before/after wording of at least one change. Multiple defensible approaches; no model answer.

## 3. C2-U08-TRANSFER-01 — Substantially revise a style-neutral editorial report

Fresh fictional source bundle: a community coastal archive is preparing an illustrated tidal atlas for public education. The atlas is historical interpretation only, not a navigation or safety guide.

A — Editorial brief: the aim is to explain how observations of changing shorelines are recorded, while distinguishing different measurement periods and uncertainty. The archive has selected twelve sketches made by volunteers at three observation points over two months. The sketches depict the visible shoreline at different times, but no standardised time-of-day or water-level record was collected. They cannot establish a long-term change in the coast.

B — Draft editorial paragraph: "Our twelve drawings prove that the shore is retreating. The volunteers' consistent observations establish the scale of the change, and the atlas will show readers the safest routes along the coast. The editor has therefore approved the completed atlas for publication."

C — Review note: the illustrations can help readers compare recorded appearances and understand why observation conditions matter. The reviewer recommends adding each sketch's known date and location, explicitly marking missing conditions and explaining why the evidence does not establish long-term change. A second reviewer worries that a dense block of technical caveats could obscure the educational purpose; they favour concise notes next to the relevant illustrations. Neither reviewer has tested reader comprehension. The two suggestions need not conflict, but layout feasibility has not been tested.

D — Production note: the editor has authorised preparation of a revised internal sample spread containing three sketches, not publication. The archive can allocate four hours of design time for that sample. Only the editorial board can approve publication following review. There is no mandate or evidence to issue route or safety guidance.

Task:
1. Write a 300–400-word style-neutral editorial report for the board, with a concise executive summary within that length. Evaluate the educational value and interpretive limits of the sketches, explain why the draft's inference and safety claims are not supported, assess the two reviewers' priorities without caricature, and describe a bounded sample-spread review consistent with resources and authority. Include what should be assessed before a publication decision; do not invent findings.
2. Draft V1, reverse-outline it, and then substantially revise to V2. In a 100–150-word commentary, quote at least two actual before/after passages; identify a structural repair (such as moving a consequential limit closer to a claim or changing the executive summary to match the body), a lexical/epistemic repair, and a trade-off you retained rather than smoothing away. Explain why the result suits a style-neutral editorial report rather than advocacy or personal reflection.
3. Write one alternative 2–3-sentence opening for a critical essay about the same material, then explain which genre functions change and which facts must not. No external research or practical coastal navigation guidance is requested.

Qualitative self-check: summary matches the body; reported observations not confused with long-term causal evidence; material uncertainty is visible at the relevant claim; two reviewers' purposes accurately represented; resources and approval preserved; no invented safety claim or publication; documented revisions are substantive; genre variation does not change evidence.
