## 1. REA-B2-10 — Read a report brief without overclaiming (`B2-U07`)

**CORE-1: Goal and context.** You are helping a school committee distinguish what a short evidence brief says from what an enthusiastic recommendation merely assumes. By the end, identify the purpose, support, limit, counterpoint and proportionate next step of an organised report. **Target:** B2; suggested 25 minutes.

**CORE-2: Teaching and independent worked demonstration.** Before drafting a report, create a five-column evidence plan: *decision / observed fact / interpretation / limitation / recommendation*. A clear report answers a concrete question, separates observation from causation and gives an actionable conditional conclusion. **Independent model, different topic from the scored passage:** A community library records 18 participants at a new evening workshop. A careful report says “18 people attended this first session” rather than “evening workshops increase literacy”; it might recommend a further session and feedback questions. **Pitfall:** turning attendance into proof of learning impact.

**CORE-3: Independent reading source (fictional).**

> **Report to the Fernhill School Council: Should the Repair Café return?**  
> In September the school ran a four-week lunchtime Repair Café, where supervised pupils tried simple repairs to bags, stationery cases and bicycle lights. The aim was practical: explore whether a staffed space would be useful, not to measure long-term sustainability outcomes. The sign-in table recorded 71 visits. Because repeat attendance was not tracked, this figure cannot be translated into 71 distinct pupils. Volunteers recorded 29 items brought in and 17 completed repairs; some items were taken away for further work, and others were unsuitable for the available tools.  
> In a short optional feedback form, 24 respondents said they would consider attending another session. That is encouraging, but the form was distributed to people already at the Café. We know little about pupils who avoided it or could not attend at lunchtime. Staff also reported a practical constraint: sessions required two adults at all times, and a few sessions began late when one adult was unavailable.  
> These findings do not establish that the Café reduced waste across the school. They show that a group made use of the service and that some basic repairs were completed. A sensible next step would be a limited second trial, with a reliable supervision rota, a count of unique visitors, a record of repair outcomes and a feedback route open to non-attenders. The school should decide on any permanent programme only after examining both participation and staff workload.

**Choose exactly one best answer per item.**

**REA-B2-10-Q1.** What is the writer's primary purpose?  
A. To demonstrate a measured reduction in school-wide waste  
B. To weigh evidence and constraints before recommending a further test  
C. To prove that every pupil can repair electronic equipment  
D. To argue that the Café should be closed immediately  
**Key:** B. **Explanation:** The report distinguishes early use from unmeasured impact and proposes a bounded second trial. **Misconception:** confusing the initial programme's aspiration with an established outcome. **Next action:** underline what was actually counted.

**REA-B2-10-Q2.** Why cannot 71 visits be reported as 71 different users?  
A. The Café opened for more than one week  
B. All pupils were surveyed anonymously  
C. The sign-in system did not identify repeat visitors  
D. Only adults could bring repair items  
**Key:** C. **Explanation:** A visit count can include repeated attendance; no person-level count was kept. **Next action:** label the unit of every figure before using it in a report.

**REA-B2-10-Q3.** Which sentence would be most defensible in the report's executive summary?  
A. The pilot completed some simple repairs, but whole-school environmental effects remain unknown.  
B. The pilot proved that future waste will be reduced by exactly 17 items a month.  
C. The voluntary respondents represented all Fernhill pupils.  
D. The school no longer needs two adults to supervise repairs.  
**Key:** A. **Explanation:** The 17 completed repairs are recorded, while longer-term or school-wide impacts were not measured. **Next action:** separate a measured output from a claimed impact.

**REA-B2-10-Q4.** Which observation most directly identifies an implementation constraint?  
A. The pilot ran for four weeks.  
B. Several pupils attended more than once.  
C. The programme had a sustainability-related aim.  
D. Sessions needed two adults and some started late when staffing fell short.  
**Key:** D. **Explanation:** Staffing demand and delayed starts affect the feasibility of repeating the programme. **Next action:** include operational costs, not only popularity, in a recommendation.

**REA-B2-10-Q5.** Which data collection step best addresses a gap named in the report?  
A. Replace the sign-in system with a claim about total school participation.  
B. Gather unique-visitor counts and invite feedback from non-attenders.  
C. Ask only pupils who completed repairs to rate their enjoyment.  
D. Stop recording repairs because output measures cannot prove impact.  
**Key:** B. **Explanation:** This tests reach and possible exclusion, two areas the first trial did not measure. **Next action:** link each proposed measure to a stated uncertainty.

**CORE-4: After-submission transfer (unscored).** Write a five-line report outline: decision, one observation, one uncertainty, one staff constraint, one conditional next step. Self-check: Have you claimed an outcome the source did not actually observe? If so, replace it with an appropriately bounded statement.

## 2. WRI-B2-08 — Plan an evidence-bound report (`B2-U07`)

**CORE-1: Goal and context.** Create a logically organised, multi-paragraph recommendation using two distinct source briefs. **Target:** B2, suggested 30–40 minutes across optional visits. This task measures practice in source-aware writing; it cannot award a B2 level.

**CORE-2: Teach the process.** (1) Parse the decision and audience. (2) Record what each source supports and cannot show. (3) Write a one-sentence qualified position. (4) Outline introduction → two evidence paragraphs → limitation/counterpoint → measured next step. (5) Draft in your own words and perform a source-check audit. **Independent demonstration, separate topic:** If a museum proposes a weekend opening trial, a report outline could place recorded attendance first, then staffing concerns, then a reversible three-week trial. The model deliberately contains no facts or language from the assessed school sports-space sources. **Pitfall:** writing a persuasive advert instead of a decision brief.

**CORE-3: Original assessed source bundle.**

**Source A — Student council survey memorandum.** The fictional Westmere School considers opening the covered sports court for supervised after-school activities twice a week. A voluntary online survey received 96 responses; 61 respondents preferred access after classes. The survey link was posted on the sports clubs' shared noticeboard, and year groups were not sampled evenly. Preferences of non-respondents are unknown.

**Source B — Facilities coordinator's note.** In a three-day trial, the court was used by between 18 and 27 pupils each afternoon. The coordinator estimates that opening twice a week would require a trained supervisor and a final equipment check, but the staffing rota is not approved. One trial day coincided with cancellation of outdoor practice because of rain. The figures do not measure exercise improvement or longer-term attendance.

**WRI-B2-08-Q1 (extended response; UNSCORED).** Write a **190–250-word** report to the Westmere School Council. Include (i) the decision under consideration, (ii) at least one accurate bounded fact from each source, (iii) two limitations or implementation risks, (iv) one fair counterargument, and (v) a conditional recommendation with two measurements for a future trial. You may recommend proceeding, postponing or rejecting the trial if your evidence is fair. Do not add research or results not supplied. Submit your own text, not a checklist.

## 3. WRI-B2-09 — Revise an extended argument with a change log (`B2-U07`)

**CORE-1: Goal.** Improve organisation and evidential accuracy in an argument by revising a flawed draft and explaining key edits. **Target:** B2; suggested 30–40 minutes.

**CORE-2: Teaching and independent model.** Run a four-pass revision: **claim strength** (match evidence), **paragraph function** (one job each), **counterargument** (fairly state it), **sentence cohesion** (meaningful links, not a connector count). **Separate worked model (different topic):** A school exhibition notice claims, “The new display has transformed everyone's understanding.” Evidence reports only 14 visitors. Revision: “Fourteen people visited the display during its first afternoon; further feedback would be needed to assess understanding.” This repairs scale without making the result disappear. **Pitfall:** treating grammar polishing as a substitute for correcting an unsupported claim.

**CORE-3: Assessed source and starter draft.**

**Source A — Fictional school media club record.** Over two weeks, 38 students attended at least one session explaining how to check the origin of a digital poster before sharing it. Twenty of these students completed an optional reflection form. The school did not measure later sharing behaviour or compare the students with a group that received no session.

**Source B — Teacher observation.** Teachers reported that discussion was lively, but some students struggled to distinguish the poster's original publisher from an account that reposted it. The media club cannot add sessions next term unless an additional teacher volunteers.

**Flawed draft to revise (deliberately unreliable):** “The media club has proved that misinformation will disappear from our school. Everybody learned to verify posters because thirty-eight students came to the sessions. Also, the workshops were fun. On the other hand, the club needs more teachers, but that does not matter because enthusiasm is enough. Therefore the school must make this programme compulsory immediately.”

**WRI-B2-09-Q1 (extended response; UNSCORED).** Write a **180–240-word** revised recommendation for a school newsletter, followed by a brief **three-point revision log** (not counted in the main word range). Keep the evidence accurate, address the staffing counterargument, state what remains unknown and propose a feasible next step. You may retain the conclusion only if appropriately qualified. Do not present your draft as proof of a proficiency level.

**Rubric safeguard:** existing `RUBRIC-WRITING-B2`, `answer:null`, `evidence_weight:0`, `review_mode:assisted_ai_or_human`; feedback must remain **UNASSESSED** until trusted review. Review the *revision decisions*, not only text fluency.

**CORE-4:** Self-check: Did you remove unsupported claims of universal learning, causality and compulsion? Did every paragraph perform a distinct function? Can another reader trace how evidence led to your qualified recommendation? Next step after trusted feedback: revise one sentence that still overstates the source.

## 4. REA-C1-02 — Trace information flow in extended discourse (`C1-U01`)

**CORE-1: Goal and context.** In a demanding extended commentary, trace how a writer introduces a problem, revises a definition, uses an apparent objection and reaches a bounded conclusion. **Target:** C1, suggested 30 minutes. Successful items are practice evidence, not a C1 certification.

**CORE-2: Teaching and independent demonstration.** Track **theme → what is new → how it becomes the next theme**, then identify referents of pronouns and summarising phrases. Ask whether “however” signals a true contradiction, a restriction of scope or a changed question. **Independent model, unrelated to the scored passage:** “The book exchange opened quietly. That modest start was not a failure; it allowed organisers to discover which shelves people actually used. The discovery changed their original layout.” Here *that modest start* summarises the previous event, and *the discovery* turns the measured experience into a reason for design change. **Pitfall:** equating coherent writing with inserting more transition words.

**CORE-3: Original scored source (fictional commentary).**

> **Who is a public square for?**  
> When the Briarfield council invited suggestions for its central square, the first submissions appeared to divide neatly into two camps. Some residents wanted more seating and quiet areas. Others requested space for performances, markets and temporary exhibitions. The council's consultation summary described these as competing uses. Yet this tidy opposition concealed a problem: each group was speaking about a different experience of the same place.  
> For older residents who crossed the square at midday, noise and a lack of shade made lingering difficult. For students who visited after classes, the square was already quiet enough, but there was little reason to stay. Neither account was false. The mistake was to treat each as a timeless description of the square rather than an observation made at a particular hour.  
> That distinction matters because planning proposals often turn measured preferences into fixed identities. A person who asks for a quieter lunchtime is labelled an opponent of cultural events; another who supports a weekend concert is assumed to reject calm public space altogether. Once those labels harden, compromise begins to look like surrender. What residents may actually dispute is not whether one activity is legitimate, but when, where and under what conditions it can occur.  
> The council therefore conducted a short mapping exercise. Volunteers recorded where people stopped, walked and gathered during selected mornings, afternoons and weekends. These observations were limited: rainy days were under-represented, and the map did not show why any person chose a particular route. Still, it revealed that the edges of the square were used differently from the central path, and that some peak activities seldom overlapped in time.  
> This finding does not settle the design. A map cannot decide whose comfort should matter most, and a single season cannot capture how uses change throughout the year. It does, however, change the question. Instead of choosing between an entirely quiet square and an event venue, the council can now examine flexible seating, shaded routes and times for temporary events. The resulting design may be less dramatic than either original proposal. Its value would lie in responding to the variety that the first summary had obscured.

**REA-C1-02-Q1.** What role does the third paragraph play in the argument?  
A. It proves that all consultation groups deliberately misreported their views.  
B. It explains how time-bound preferences can be misleadingly recast as fixed opposing identities.  
C. It introduces a historical account of the square's construction.  
D. It rejects the possibility of any shared use of public space.  
**Key:** B. **Feedback:** The paragraph develops the consequences of a mistaken framing, not a claim of dishonesty. **Next:** Identify how the second paragraph's observation becomes a general planning warning.

**REA-C1-02-Q2.** In “That distinction matters”, what distinction is being carried forward?  
A. The difference between an observation at a particular time and a permanent description of a place  
B. The difference between volunteers and government employees  
C. The difference between historical buildings and modern benches  
D. The difference between indoor and outdoor performances  
**Key:** A. **Feedback:** The previous paragraph states that opposing impressions arose from different times and conditions. **Next:** Resolve summary phrases by reading the preceding paragraph's main contrast.

**REA-C1-02-Q3.** Which statement best describes the function of the mapping exercise?  
A. It removed the need for further decisions about competing preferences.  
B. It proved that concerts had no impact on other users.  
C. It provided limited spatial and temporal evidence that could reframe design options.  
D. It represented every season and every possible use of the square.  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** Mapping uncovers patterns while remaining limited in weather coverage, interpretation and time span. **Next:** Separate a tool's useful contribution from what it cannot determine.

**REA-C1-02-Q4.** What does “It does, however, change the question” accomplish?  
A. It dismisses the consultation as worthless.  
B. It claims that a map is an objective way to decide public priorities.  
C. It moves from choosing between two rigid options to examining conditional combinations.  
D. It announces that residents must withdraw their original requests.  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** The writer keeps the original concerns but revises the decision frame. **Next:** Trace whether each paragraph changes the evidence, the question or the conclusion.

**REA-C1-02-Q5.** Which qualification most directly limits the exercise's representativeness?  
A. Volunteers recorded routes and gathering places.  
B. Rainy days were under-represented, and one season cannot capture annual variation.  
C. The square has both a central path and outer edges.  
D. Some submissions preferred performances.  
**Key:** B. **Feedback:** Weather and season limit the generalisability of the observations. **Next:** Name who, when or what is missing from a purportedly comprehensive claim.

**REA-C1-02-Q6.** Which interpretation best captures the final paragraph's rhetorical movement?  
A. From an absolute choice to a qualified, evidence-responsive design approach  
B. From residents' needs to a demand for maximum event revenue  
C. From modest mapping evidence to proof of universal agreement  
D. From environmental conditions to rejection of temporary events  
**Key:** A. **Feedback:** The conclusion proposes flexible arrangements without pretending the trade-offs vanish. **Next:** Check whether the conclusion is stronger than the evidence that precedes it.

**CORE-4: Unscored synthesis.** In 90–120 words, explain how “that distinction”, “this finding” and “change the question” connect the argument. Review feedback should flag any unsupported claim of consensus; practise with a new text before inferring C1 competence.

## 5. GRA-C1-01 — Control reference, emphasis and qualification (`C1-U01`)

**CORE-1:** Choose structures that preserve exact reference, information focus and strength of argument in extended formal prose. **Target:** C1; approximately 25 minutes. Although listed under Grammar, this lesson assesses discourse-sensitive grammar, not rare structures for their own sake.

**CORE-2: Meaning, structure and worked example.** A cohesive text must make clear **what “this/these/such a claim” refers to**, place familiar information before the next significant point when useful, and distinguish concessive scope (“although”, “even so”) from a complete reversal. Cleft structures can place an intended element into focus; they do not supply evidence. **Independent model (different from active stems):** “The archive has digitised 400 photographs. This change improves access, although the descriptions remain incomplete. It is the missing context, rather than the image quality, that now needs attention.” The reference *this change* points to digitisation; *although* adds a limitation; the cleft highlights a specific unresolved issue. **Pitfall:** vague “this proves it” when the antecedent or proof is unclear.

**CORE-3: Independent multiple-choice tasks.** Choose the most coherent and faithful version in each setting; all facts in the prompts are fictional.

**GRA-C1-01-Q1.** A report states, “Three small workshops attracted returning participants, but the organiser did not track unique visitors.” Which continuation makes the reference most precise?  
A. This proves the workshops improved everyone's skills.  
B. Such a limitation makes it impossible to report any attendance.  
C. This missing distinction between visits and individuals limits claims about reach.  
D. These individuals therefore attended precisely once each.  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** The sentence names the exact antecedent and the inferential limit. **Next:** Replace vague pronouns with the relevant noun phrase when multiple referents compete.

**GRA-C1-01-Q2.** Which sentence accurately gives focus to the *location*, not the timetable, of a proposed display?  
A. It was beside the west entrance that the committee proposed placing the display.  
B. It was next Friday that the committee proposed placing the display.  
C. It was the committee that proposed the display in every building.  
D. It was the display that proposed placing the committee at the west entrance.  
**Key:** A. **Feedback:** The cleft foregrounds the west-entrance location without changing the underlying event. **Next:** Ask which constituent the writer intends to contrast.

**GRA-C1-01-Q3.** A pilot records fewer late arrivals, but it also ran during an unusual exam timetable. Which sentence preserves both facts without claiming causation?  
A. Since the pilot eliminated lateness, the timetable no longer matters.  
B. Although the pilot coincided with fewer late arrivals, the unusual timetable makes the result difficult to interpret.  
C. The pilot failed because fewer students were late.  
D. In spite of there being no recorded arrivals, lateness was certainly reduced.  
**Key:** B. **Feedback:** The concession preserves the observation and adds its methodological limitation. **Next:** Check that a linker relates propositions that the evidence actually supports.

**GRA-C1-01-Q4.** Which continuation best links the old information to the newly important question? “A review team found inconsistent labels on three exhibits.”  
A. Such discrepancies raised a new question: who should check the labels before opening?  
B. Such a discrepancy means every exhibit was accurately labelled.  
C. It arrived by bus at noon.  
D. They were not available to anyone because a new question was a bicycle.  
**Key:** A. **Feedback:** “Such discrepancies” gathers the previous finding and turns it into a process question. **Next:** Prefer precise anaphoric nouns over empty “this/it”.

**GRA-C1-01-Q5.** A review finds that the **sample**, not the questionnaire's wording, is the main weakness. Which sentence best expresses that focus?  
A. What the review primarily questions is the sample, rather than the questionnaire's wording.  
B. What the sample primarily questions is the wording's review.  
C. There is no weakness because the questionnaire has words.  
D. It is the wording that is the only issue, not the sample.  
**Key:** A. **Feedback:** A pseudo-cleft selects the correct contrast without reversing the claim. **Next:** Read a focus structure backwards as a plain sentence to verify meaning.

**GRA-C1-01-Q6.** Which revision most improves the logical relation? “The park trial attracted visitors. It did not show how regularly the same people returned. The council wants a longer study.”  
A. “The trial attracted visitors, therefore it proved permanent demand, although the council wants evidence.”  
B. “The trial attracted visitors; even so, because repeat attendance was not measured, the council wants a longer study.”  
C. “Although the council wants a longer study, nobody visited the trial.”  
D. “The trial was successful, success is success, and therefore there is nothing to test.”  
**Key:** B. **Feedback:** The revision makes the evidence gap the reason for further study without denying attendance. **Next:** Explain each connector's actual logical work before selecting it.

**CORE-4:** Provide item-specific explanations and a short unscored edit of one paragraph from a different text. Avoid calling knowledge of six forms a validated C1 writing ability.

## 6. WRI-C1-01 — Build a coherent briefing from disordered evidence (`C1-U01`)

**CORE-1:** Build a well-signposted extended explanation that distinguishes observed patterns, potential explanations, competing priorities and a tentative decision. **Target:** C1; 35–45 minutes, potentially across visits. No automatic semantic grade.

**CORE-2: Teach and model.** Plan the **argument path** before writing: central decision → current evidence → interpretation and alternatives → genuine uncertainty → qualified recommendation. Introduce the next paragraph with a specific backward link such as “That uneven coverage” rather than a decorative “Furthermore”. **Independent worked model (separate topic):** A local science centre counted 52 visitors at a short astronomy display but surveyed only participants. A 3-sentence executive outline would report the count, identify the selection issue and request a second measurement period before a permanent recommendation. **Pitfall:** stacking facts without showing why the next paragraph follows.

**CORE-3: Disordered evidence notes (all fictional; rearrange and interpret, do not invent new measurements):**

1. The Birchbank School recorded courtyard temperatures on six sunny afternoons, at two shaded benches and two exposed tables. At the recorded times, the shaded benches averaged roughly 3°C cooler than the exposed tables; measurement locations and times were limited.
2. Some students proposed additional shade sails. Others worried that fixed structures might limit activities and require maintenance.
3. The six-day comparison does not show year-round conditions or how students would choose to use the space after changes.
4. The facilities team says the proposed structures would need a safety assessment and a maintenance budget; neither has been approved.
5. The student council needs a recommendation about whether to investigate a reversible pilot rather than approve permanent construction immediately.
6. A separate optional suggestion board received comments from 31 pupils, but the council cannot treat those comments as representative of all year groups.

**WRI-C1-01-Q1 (extended response; UNSCORED).** Write a **230–320-word briefing** for the Birchbank council, using the six notes in an order you defend. Present a clear decision question, report the limited temperature observations, compare the competing priorities, acknowledge sampling and safety/budget limits, and recommend an evidence-gathering next step. Use meaningful cross-paragraph reference, not a fixed number of linking expressions. Add a one-sentence explanation of your most important paragraph transition below the briefing.

## 7. REA-C1-03 — Infer a commentator's qualified position (`C1-U02`)

**CORE-1:** Distinguish what a writer states, implies, questions and deliberately leaves unsettled in extended commentary. **Target:** C1; 30 minutes. Infer only what textual evidence warrants.

**CORE-2: Teaching and worked model.** Mark five categories: **stated fact / speaker's interpretation / concession / unresolved question / unsupported reader addition**. Position can be conveyed by selecting a concern, not only by stating “I support” or “I oppose”. **Independent miniature model, different topic:** “The new school garden has attracted volunteers. Whether it will survive the dry season is another matter.” The writer accepts early engagement but withholds confidence in durability; the statement does not predict failure. **Pitfall:** treating cautious language as secretly absolute opposition.

**CORE-3: Original editorial (fictional).**

> **Should the school newspaper accept anonymous feedback?**  
> The editor of the Ashmere Student Journal recently suggested that readers should be able to send criticism without attaching their names. The idea appeals to anyone who has watched a student remain silent in a meeting, not because the comment lacks merit but because a public disagreement may carry social consequences. An anonymous channel could lower that particular barrier. It might also expose the editors to allegations they cannot verify.  
> The proposal is therefore often discussed as a contest between courage and accountability. That framing is convenient, but it smuggles in a misleading assumption: that attaching a name always makes a claim reliable, while withholding one makes it suspect. A named submission can be inaccurate, just as an anonymous one can document a real problem. The editor's task is not to reward one kind of speaker automatically. It is to decide what can responsibly be published.  
> Consider a complaint that the music room is consistently locked before its advertised closing time. The issue is potentially checkable: a reporter can observe several afternoons, consult the published schedule and seek a response from staff. The complainant's identity may be less important than the evidence. Now consider a personal accusation against a student that cannot be independently corroborated. An anonymous label cannot turn an allegation into a publishable fact; nor would a signature necessarily do so.  
> A well-run channel would separate receiving information from printing it. Editors could accept anonymous tips, ask for supporting details, and withhold publication until the material meets the same standards applied to named claims. They would also need to explain, carefully, why some messages are not published. Otherwise readers might mistake silence for indifference or publication for endorsement of every assertion.  
> None of this makes the decision effortless. Verifying a claim can take time, and students who offer credible information may still fear that a follow-up question will identify them. Yet rejecting anonymity outright would not remove those vulnerabilities; it would largely make them less visible. The more useful question is whether the Journal can build a process that protects contributors while resisting unsupported accusations. That process would need testing, training and clear limits before anyone could responsibly call it successful.

**REA-C1-03-Q1.** Which account of the writer's position is best supported?  
A. The writer wants all anonymous accusations printed immediately.  
B. The writer rejects every form of unnamed feedback.  
C. The writer conditionally supports receiving anonymous tips while requiring verification before publication.  
D. The writer argues that a signature makes a claim automatically true.  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** The proposed distinction is between accepting tips and publishing claims under normal standards. **Next:** Compare the proposed process, not an isolated warning.

**REA-C1-03-Q2.** Why does the writer challenge the “courage and accountability” framing?  
A. It assumes that reliability can be inferred simply from whether a name is attached.  
B. It treats all student opinions as equally factually correct.

## 8. REA-C1-04 — Compare two defensible interpretations (`C1-U02`)

**CORE-1:** Compare two related texts without collapsing different speaker purposes into a simple “for/against” verdict. Distinguish overlap, rhetorical emphasis, limits and unsupported inference. **Target:** C1, suggested 30 minutes.

**CORE-2: Strategy and separate worked example.** Make an **interpretation table**: *shared concern / explicit difference / phrase supporting each view / inference ceiling*. **Independent model, different topic:** Writer A says a town's new mural “has given residents something to discuss”; Writer B writes that the mural “has yet to bring everyone into the conversation.” Both can recognise its visibility while differing about participation. Neither statement proves the mural is worthless. **Pitfall:** turning a difference of emphasis into a contradiction of every factual claim.

**CORE-3: Original paired sources (fictional).**

**Source A — Student council chair's public statement**  
> Our school introduced a quiet hour in the library for three weeks, reserving a room for silent individual work after lunch. The first attendance sheets show that some pupils used the room regularly, although the sheets cannot tell us why they came or whether they worked more effectively. For the next round I favour maintaining a clearly identified quiet period. Several pupils have said that they struggle to find a calm corner, and the school ought to test ways of meeting that need. At the same time, the limited data cannot justify closing the library's shared-work tables throughout the afternoon. A careful pilot would offer both spaces and review their use separately.

**Source B — School librarian's reflective note**  
> The quiet hour has been described as though the library were previously either silent or chaotic. In fact, its atmosphere changes with the day and the activity. Some students need concentration; others learn by speaking through an idea with a partner. A room set aside for silence may be welcome, but it is not evidence that collaboration is a lesser form of study. Before we expand any restriction, I would like to know whether access is fair across year groups and whether the new arrangement shifts noise into corridors. I am not against a quiet space. I am against treating a single measure of attendance as a complete account of what a library is for.

**REA-C1-04-Q1.** Where is the clearest substantive agreement?  
A. Both think only silent work deserves school resources.  
B. Both acknowledge a possible role for quiet space while resisting conclusions beyond the evidence.  
C. Both claim the pilot measured improvements in grades.  
D. Both demand immediate closure of group-work tables.  
**Key:** B. **Feedback:** Each accepts a potential benefit and raises limits. **Next:** Name agreement before isolating differences in emphasis.

**REA-C1-04-Q2.** What is Source B most concerned about?  
A. Preserving multiple legitimate learning functions and testing indirect effects of restrictions  
B. Making quiet study unavailable to all pupils  
C. Proving that collaboration always raises exam scores  
D. Replacing all attendance sheets with staff impressions  
**Key:** A. **Feedback:** The librarian questions a reductive account of learning and asks about equity and displacement. **Next:** Distinguish the writer's concern from a categorical policy demand.

**REA-C1-04-Q3.** Which sentence most clearly shows that Source A rejects an extreme restriction?  
A. “Our school introduced a quiet hour in the library for three weeks.”  
B. “Several pupils have said that they struggle to find a calm corner.”  
C. “A careful pilot would offer both spaces and review their use separately.”  
D. “The first attendance sheets show that some pupils used the room regularly.”  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** The proposed continuation protects both quiet and shared work and evaluates them separately. **Next:** Find each author's concrete proposal, not only their criticism.

**REA-C1-04-Q4.** Which inference about the original pilot is justified across both sources?  
A. It demonstrated that all pupils learn best in silence.  
B. It established that quiet study worsens corridor noise.  
C. It produced early observations but left effects and reasons for use uncertain.  
D. It failed because students refused to attend.  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** Both sources treat attendance as insufficient to infer learning effects or longer-term consequences. **Next:** Separate what a study records from what people might wish it proved.

**REA-C1-04-Q5.** Which interpretation **overstates** a difference between the speakers?  
A. The chair proposes a continued quiet period with shared-space safeguards.  
B. The librarian stresses that a library serves different styles of study.  
C. The chair and librarian fundamentally disagree over whether any quiet space should exist.  
D. Both want better information before wider restrictions.  
**Key:** C. **Feedback:** The librarian explicitly says they are not against quiet space; both can support a bounded version. **Next:** Test your contrast against disconfirming phrases.

**REA-C1-04-Q6.** Which follow-up measure would most directly address a concern distinctive to Source B?  
A. Counting only how many pupils enter the quiet room  
B. Measuring corridor noise and access across year groups alongside room use  
C. Asking quiet-room users whether they like having a timetable  
D. Printing more copies of the council chair's statement  
**Key:** B. **Feedback:** Source B calls attention to displacement and unequal access, not simply attendance. **Next:** Tie the measure to the author's specific uncertainty.

**CORE-4:** Independent unscored task: Write two defensible sentences about a genuine difference of emphasis, with a quotation or tightly paraphrased phrase from each source, followed by one sentence explaining what neither source establishes. A trusted reviewer may discuss alternative interpretations if they are anchored to the texts.
