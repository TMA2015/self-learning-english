## 1. VOC-C1-01 — Precise collocations when evaluating evidence

### CORE-1: Goal and context

**Learner goal:** Select natural, precise word combinations when discussing what evidence shows, what it does not establish, and what a project team should examine next. In a student research committee, words such as *draw a distinction*, *establish a link* or *cast doubt on* help writers express relationships accurately. A collocation is a conventional combination, not simply any grammatically possible verb–noun pairing. **Task:** Choose one best option in each of six unfamiliar sentences. Suggested time: 20–25 minutes. Advisory bridge: B2-U02.

### CORE-2: Explicit teaching and independent worked model

1. **Read for purpose before choosing vocabulary.** Is the sentence reporting evidence, evaluating uncertainty, making a decision, or describing consequences? Collocations encode these relationships.
2. **Check the whole pattern.** A word may be grammatical but unnatural with the noun or preposition: e.g., *call into question* (not *call to question* when expressing doubt), *have implications for* (not always *implications on*).
3. **Check strength.** *Suggests* does not mean *demonstrates*; *may constrain* does not mean *will prevent*. Keep the degree of confidence in the original context.
4. **Try a plain-English paraphrase.** If the combination cannot be paraphrased without changing the claim, reconsider it.

**Independent worked example (NOT an assessed stem):** A student writes, “The rehearsal feedback **makes attention to** an accessibility issue.” The intended meaning is that the feedback directs people's attention to a problem. The natural revision is “The rehearsal feedback **draws attention to** an accessibility issue.” *Makes attention to* is not the conventional expression. This says only that an issue has been noticed; it does not claim that the issue is widespread or proven to be caused by the rehearsal.

**Unscored self-check:** In your own words, explain why “the observation proves the cause” would usually be stronger than “the observation points to a possible cause.” A suitable answer distinguishes proof from a plausible explanation; this check is not an MCQ answer key.

### CORE-3: Independent objective practice

**Directions:** Each item describes a distinct part of an invented community-library research project. Select the **single best conventional expression** that fits both the wording and the meaning. These are new assessed stems, separate from the worked example.

**VOC-C1-01-Q1** — `vocabulary.collocations`  
The committee needs to ______ between the number of bookings and the number of different people using the study rooms; one person can book repeatedly.

A. make an allowance  
B. draw a distinction  
C. take exception  
D. strike a balance

**Key: B.** **Rationale:** *Draw a distinction between X and Y* is the natural conventional pattern for making a conceptual difference explicit. **Misconception:** choosing an established collocation (*strike a balance*, *take exception*) that has a different communicative purpose; this sentence requires distinguishing measures, not reconciling them or objecting. **Repair:** pair the verb with the full *distinction between* pattern and paraphrase as “treat these measures as different.”

**VOC-C1-01-Q2** — `vocabulary.collocations`  
A short pilot may suggest that the new booking page is useful, but it cannot by itself ______ between the redesign and later attendance.

A. establish a causal link  
B. inaugurate a causal link  
C. manufacture a causal link  
D. administer a causal link

**Key: A.** **Rationale:** *Establish a causal link between X and Y* describes the level of evidence needed to support a cause-and-effect claim; one pilot does not necessarily provide it. **Misconception:** mistaking a general action verb for the conventionally used research expression. **Repair:** underline *causal link between* and distinguish evidence of a relationship from proof of its cause.

**VOC-C1-01-Q3** — `vocabulary.collocations`  
New records show that many registrations were never followed by a visit. This ______ the assumption that registration totals equal actual attendance.

A. lends weight to  
B. draws a parallel with  
C. casts doubt on  
D. sets aside

**Key: C.** **Rationale:** *Cast doubt on an assumption* naturally means provide grounds for questioning it, not necessarily disprove every part of it. **Misconception:** recognising a natural expression but ignoring its direction: *lends weight to* would strengthen the assumption, while *sets aside* would claim a more decisive rejection than the evidence warrants. **Repair:** practise *cast doubt on X* and explain what remains unresolved.

**VOC-C1-01-Q4** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
The library can recruit only two assistants next month, so staffing limits may ______ the scope of the follow-up survey.

A. endorse  
B. exaggerate  
C. guarantee  
D. constrain

**Key: D.** **Rationale:** *Constrain the scope* means restrict how much can realistically be done, matching limited staff resources. **Misconception:** treating any formal-sounding verb as appropriate regardless of what the sentence says. **Repair:** paraphrase “may limit the survey's range” before choosing the verb.

**VOC-C1-01-Q5** — `vocabulary.collocations`  
Because the interviews involved only volunteers from one branch, the evidence ______ a more cautious interpretation than the publicity leaflet offers.

A. manufactures  
B. warrants  
C. enforces  
D. administers

**Key: B.** **Rationale:** *Evidence warrants an interpretation* means that the evidence justifies that way of understanding the findings; the small non-representative group calls for caution. **Misconception:** treating evidence as if it physically forces an interpretation. **Repair:** rephrase as “the evidence justifies a cautious reading,” without claiming total certainty.

**VOC-C1-01-Q6** — `vocabulary.collocations`  
Before committing to longer opening hours, the team will ______ the implications for staff workload and room security.

A. explore  
B. expose  
C. occupy  
D. perform

**Key: A.** **Rationale:** *Explore the implications for X* means examine potential consequences; the decision is not yet final. **Misconception:** equating superficially similar verbs beginning with *ex-* or general action verbs. **Repair:** substitute “investigate the possible consequences for staff and security” and test the full phrase.

**Author-proposed objective key sequence:** `BACDBA`.

## 2. VOC-C1-02 — Register and audience without distorting meaning

### CORE-1: Goal and context

**Learner goal:** Recognise the version of a message that best fits a named audience and purpose **while keeping the original facts, promise and uncertainty unchanged**. A student committee may communicate with teammates, families and officials about the same project; an effective shift in register changes wording, not evidence. **Task:** Choose the best revision or phrase for six different audiences. Suggested time: 20–30 minutes. Advisory bridge: B2-U02.

### CORE-2: Explicit teaching and independent worked model

1. Identify **who** receives the message, **why** it is sent and what the reader must do or understand.
2. Preserve the **claim** and any scope limits: do not turn a tentative plan into a confirmed result, or a limited problem into a crisis.
3. Choose degree of formality: simple and warm for peers; clear and respectful for families; precise and neutral for official reports. Higher register is not automatically better.
4. Check accessibility and agency: avoid jargon that hides responsibility; avoid needless judgement about readers or users.

**Independent worked example (NOT assessed):** Informal draft to a student volunteer: “Can you send me your bit when you get a sec?” In a request to a partner organisation, a more suitable version is “Could you send your section by Thursday afternoon so that we can finalise the joint report?” This is clearer about the action and deadline. It would **not** be acceptable to change the request into “Your failure to send your section has delayed the report,” because the original contained no allegation of failure. Formal tone without factual fidelity is still poor writing.

**Unscored self-check:** Which matters more when rewriting for a new reader: using complicated vocabulary or preserving the message and choosing an appropriate tone? Explain your choice. A suitable response prioritises fidelity, clarity and audience.

### CORE-3: Independent objective practice

**Directions:** Choose the **single best** wording for the stated reader and purpose, preserving facts and certainty. Alternative wordings may be grammatically possible; the audience, purpose and source fact determine the key.

**VOC-C1-02-Q1** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
**Source fact:** Applications for the after-school workshop will be confirmed on Friday, not Wednesday, because the team needs extra time. **Audience:** Families waiting for a clear public update. Which message is best?

A. “The workshop will now take place on Friday, as extra time is needed to check the applications.”  
B. “Confirmations will be sent on Friday rather than Wednesday while we complete the review. Thank you for your patience.”  
C. “Application decisions have been postponed indefinitely while we investigate administrative problems.”  
D. “We will confirm accepted applications on Wednesday and contact unsuccessful applicants on Friday.”

**Key: B.** **Rationale:** It states the revised confirmation day and reason without inventing acceptance or a crisis; tone suits families. **Misconception:** equating formality with exaggerated or evasive language. **Repair:** preserve *what changed, why, what happens next* in plain professional language.

**VOC-C1-02-Q2** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
**Source fact:** Only twelve people replied to a voluntary survey; their responses cannot represent all 600 members. **Audience:** An evidence-focused project report. Which revision is the most accurate?

A. “The twelve responses reveal how the membership as a whole views the service.”  
B. “The small response group reported a clear consensus across all 600 members.”  
C. “The twelve voluntary responses offer useful examples but do not justify conclusions about all members.”  
D. “The voluntary sample is broadly representative of members who did not reply.”

**Key: C.** **Rationale:** It retains the limited evidential scope and neutral report tone. **Misconception:** allowing a polished sentence to convert a convenience sample into representative evidence. **Repair:** name the actual respondent group and the population not reliably covered.

**VOC-C1-02-Q3** — `vocabulary.collocations`  
**Audience:** Student volunteers already helping with the festival. **Purpose:** Send a friendly, concise message asking whether they are free on Saturday. Choose the best sentence.

A. “Could you confirm whether you're available on Saturday? It will help us finalise the rota.”  
B. “Please submit your attendance record for Saturday's shift.”  
C. “If you cannot attend your Saturday shift, you must arrange a replacement.”  
D. “Your Saturday shift is confirmed; please be there on time.”

**Key: A.** **Rationale:** *Confirm whether you're available* is clear and courteous without inventing a response. **Misconception:** assuming official-sounding verbs are more polite or that the request grants consent. **Repair:** check whether the wording actually asks the recipient a question.

**VOC-C1-02-Q4** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
**Source fact:** The team examined estimated costs and agreed to trial a smaller version. **Audience:** Formal meeting minutes. Which sentence is best?

A. “After reviewing the costs, the team approved the full-scale project.”  
B. “The team judged the proposed project too costly and decided against any trial.”  
C. “The team confirmed final expenditure and guaranteed a cost-free smaller version.”  
D. “After reviewing the estimated costs, the team agreed to conduct a smaller-scale trial.”

**Key: D.** **Rationale:** The phrasing suits minutes and preserves both the tentative cost estimates and actual decision. **Misconception:** introducing a rejection or financial guarantee not contained in the source fact. **Repair:** check the nouns *estimated costs* and *trial*, then check that the action is the same.

**VOC-C1-02-Q5** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
**Audience:** A teammate who knows you well. **Purpose:** Request a quick check of an unfinished draft today, without implying a formal review. Which message best fits?

A. “Please conduct a comprehensive audit of the draft by the close of business.”  
B. “Could you certify that the draft meets every requirement before I send it?”  
C. “I have circulated the final version for your formal approval.”  
D. “Could you give the draft a quick read today and flag anything unclear?”

**Key: D.** **Rationale:** It is appropriately direct and friendly and asks for a limited informal review. **Misconception:** confusing politeness with maximum formality or implying that review already occurred. **Repair:** match the requested effort, relationship and actual stage of work.

**VOC-C1-02-Q6** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
**Source fact:** Some users liked the new check-in process, while others reported problems. **Audience:** A balanced public project update. Which sentence is best?

A. “The new process worked well for most users, although a small minority reported issues.”  
B. “Feedback on the new check-in process was mixed; we are reviewing the problems reported.”  
C. “A few users experienced problems, so the team has decided to abandon the entire process.”  
D. “We have resolved all reported difficulties, and every user is now satisfied.”

**Key: B.** **Rationale:** *Mixed feedback* reflects both reactions without dismissing complaints or claiming success/failure for everyone. **Misconception:** presenting unknown proportions, invented decisions or completed fixes as facts instead of stating the available mixed evidence. **Repair:** retain both positive and negative experiences without assigning blame.

**Author-proposed objective key sequence:** `BCADDB`.

## 3. VOC-C1-03 — Connotation, stance and figurative language in context

### CORE-1: Goal and context

**Learner goal:** Interpret what a writer's lexical choice implies beyond basic dictionary meaning, including a cautious stance, negative connotation and a metaphor's limits. Writers may say a plan is *promising*, *tentative*, *overambitious* or a *stopgap*. These are not interchangeable evaluations. **Task:** Read six unfamiliar micro-contexts and choose the best interpretation or cautious revision. Suggested time: 20–30 minutes.

### CORE-2: Explicit teaching and independent worked model

1. **Locate a phrase in its context.** Ask who is speaking, about which decision, and with what level of certainty.
2. **Separate basic denotation from attitude.** *Promising* indicates potential, not success; *controversial* tells us disagreement exists, not who is correct.
3. **Treat figures of speech as framing, not literal facts.** An author may call something a *turning point* without having evidence of long-term effects.
4. **Test an alternative paraphrase.** Does it keep the writer's reservations, or accidentally change cautious agreement into endorsement?

**Independent worked example (NOT an assessed phrase):** In a discussion of a limited weekend bus trial, a columnist describes the plan as “a welcome first step, though hardly a complete solution.” This is **qualified support**: the writer welcomes an initial improvement but explicitly rejects the claim that the problem is solved. The phrase *first step* is figurative; it cannot show that any later steps have been approved. A distractor such as “the columnist believes the entire problem is solved” ignores the qualification.

**Unscored self-check:** Write a plain-language paraphrase for “an encouraging sign, but not definitive evidence.” Preserve both the positive indication and the lack of certainty.

### CORE-3: Independent objective practice

**Directions:** Each item supplies the context needed to interpret a lexical choice. Choose **one best-supported** interpretation or revision. Do not assign a meaning stronger than the wording warrants.

**VOC-C1-03-Q1** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
In an interim report, the analyst writes: “The early figures are **indicative**, rather than conclusive, of a change in travel preferences.” Here, **indicative** most nearly means:

A. a confirmed trend across multiple observation periods  
B. too anecdotal to be worth considering  
C. suggestive but not decisive  
D. sufficient on its own to establish the cause

**Key: C.** **Rationale:** The contrast with *rather than conclusive* makes the tentative meaning explicit. **Misconception:** replacing cautious evidence with proof. **Repair:** preserve both “points toward” and “does not settle.”

**VOC-C1-03-Q2** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
The proposal received a **guarded welcome** from local shopkeepers, who liked its aim but remained worried about costs. Which interpretation is most accurate?

A. They were cautiously positive but had reservations.  
B. They gave broad approval and offered only minor practical suggestions.  
C. They would support the plan only if somebody else covered every cost.  
D. They expressed neutral interest without any sign of support.

**Key: A.** **Rationale:** *Guarded* modifies welcome to show restraint, and the following clause specifies reservations. **Misconception:** reading *welcome* as unqualified approval or *guarded* literally as physical security. **Repair:** link both halves of the sentence before paraphrasing.

**VOC-C1-03-Q3** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
A manager describes the temporary paper-sign system as a **stopgap** while the permanent display is being repaired. A stopgap here is:

A. a trial method chosen as the preferred long-term policy  
B. a temporary measure that removes the need for any repairs  
C. a binding permanent replacement for the display  
D. a short-term measure pending a more durable arrangement

**Key: D.** **Rationale:** *Stopgap* denotes an interim measure, which fits the explicitly ongoing repair. **Misconception:** treating temporary accommodation as a permanent policy. **Repair:** identify the immediate function and the longer-term unresolved need.

**VOC-C1-03-Q4** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
After defending a tight deadline, the chair **conceded** that the initial timetable had been unrealistic, but she still supported the project. What does **conceded** signal?

A. She agreed to proceed only if the project's aims were reduced.  
B. She acknowledged a criticism despite having defended her position.  
C. She withdrew her support for the entire project.  
D. She expressed regret while insisting the original deadline was realistic.

**Key: B.** **Rationale:** *Concede* here means admit a particular point reluctantly or after resistance, not abandon every related position. **Misconception:** turning limited acknowledgement into complete withdrawal. **Repair:** name exactly which point she accepted and which position she retained.

**VOC-C1-03-Q5** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
Two reviewers agree that the plan has ambitious goals. One adds that it is **overambitious** given the small team and six-week deadline. What does the added prefix convey here?

A. The goals may exceed what is realistically achievable.  
B. The goals are demanding but remain practical with enough effort.  
C. The revised plan has more ambitious targets than the previous version.  
D. The team has already failed to meet every objective.

**Key: A.** **Rationale:** *Overambitious* adds a negative judgement about feasibility, not proof of failure or completion. **Misconception:** treating near-related adjectives as having identical evaluative force. **Repair:** paraphrase *ambitious* and *overambitious* separately and compare the speaker's stance.

**VOC-C1-03-Q6** — `vocabulary.meaning_in_context`  
A headline calls a new evening timetable “a **game changer**,” but the accompanying report describes only a two-week trial with no longer-term data. Which rewrite most responsibly reports what is known?

A. “The timetable has permanently transformed the service for all users.”  
B. “The trial has proved that evening services outperform daytime services.”  
C. “The trial may affect how some people use the service, but its longer-term impact remains uncertain.”  
D. “The trial had no effect whatsoever because there are no long-term data.”

**Key: C.** **Rationale:** The rewrite replaces an unproven dramatic metaphor with a possible limited effect and explicit uncertainty. **Misconception:** treating a headline's persuasive language as measured outcome, or interpreting missing long-term evidence as proof of no effect. **Repair:** distinguish rhetorical framing, observed information, and still-unknown consequences.

**Author-proposed objective key sequence:** `CADBAC`.
