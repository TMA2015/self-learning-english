A: You've moved part of the feedback process from written comments to brief consultations. The trial report says students asked fewer follow-up questions afterwards. Is that an indication that the consultations are simply more effective than written feedback?
B: It is one possible interpretation, although I'd put the comparison slightly differently. A written comment remains available when a student returns to a draft two days later; a conversation can allow the student to question a suggestion immediately. We were trying to understand when each form of feedback is useful. Counting later questions gives us a small part of that picture.
A: But surely fewer questions means there was less confusion. Otherwise why would you have introduced the consultations in the first place?
B: Fewer recorded questions means exactly that. A student may have resolved a difficulty during a consultation, but another may have decided not to raise a further question. We did not collect the same information about both students' reasons. I would rather not let the convenience of a count make that count a substitute for the thing we want to understand.
A: So are you suggesting the trial hasn't told you very much?
B: Not about a general improvement in understanding, no. It has told us something more specific. Several students who attended said that discussing one unclear comment helped them decide what to do next. Other students said they wanted to retain a written record so they could revisit the advice. Those reports are useful for planning a subsequent version, even though they do not tell us how many students improved their work as a result.
A: If both forms have a purpose, the obvious answer would be to offer every student a full consultation and a full set of written comments.
B: That might be desirable in an ideal timetable. The issue in this particular department is how to use the same allocation of staff time. We could test written comments with a brief optional clarification slot, especially for students who cannot identify a next step. I wouldn't promise identical access to individual consultations until we know what scheduling is feasible.
A: Then your position is that a consultation is a supplement rather than a replacement?
B: For the next limited trial, yes. I would not turn that into a general rule for every subject or every student. The aim is to see whether students can explain and carry out a next revision step, and to collect feedback from those who do not request a consultation. If we learn that the arrangement leaves some students with unresolved difficulties, that would be a reason to reconsider it.
