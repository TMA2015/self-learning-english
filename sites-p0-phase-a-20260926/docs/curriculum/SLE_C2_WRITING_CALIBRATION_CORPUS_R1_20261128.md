## Case A — lantern exhibition (analogue WRI-C2-01)
### Source bundle
A community lantern exhibition is preparing a display of designs. Four makers gave written support for displaying their own sketches, subject to approving the final captions; they were not asked about the whole exhibition. The copy editor describes the draft caption as "rather broad" and "quite repairable" and asks that support be attributed only to the four conditional responses. She neither alleges dishonesty nor opposes the exhibition. The team may draft a revised caption for internal review now. Only the exhibition manager can authorise internal mounting preparation; the manager has agreed to consider this but has not approved it. The rights coordinator alone can confirm public display after the final captions are accepted. No public display or announcement is approved.
### Task
Collaborative message (120–170 words) and formal decision record (80–120 words). Preserve all invariant facts and status, invite focused repair, make responsibilities clear. Add three contrasts (implication, commitment, interpersonal effect), then revise an expression and explain why.
### V1 — collaborative message
Thank you for the work on the lantern captions. The four makers have expressed conditional support for their own sketches, rather than for the exhibition as a whole, so I suggest that we narrow the caption to reflect that distinction. The editor considers its current wording rather broad but repairable. We can draft a more precise caption for internal review now and send it to the copy editor for comment. The exhibition manager has agreed to consider internal mounting preparation, so we should be ready to start once that discussion is complete. In the meantime, please keep public announcements and display arrangements on hold. The rights coordinator can confirm public use only after the final captions are accepted. Could we compare the revised wording with the four responses before taking any further steps?
### V1 — decision record
Four makers expressed support for displaying their own sketches, conditional on approval of the final captions. Their views on the wider exhibition were not requested. The copy editor considered the caption rather broad but repairable and requested a narrower attribution. A revised caption may be drafted for internal review. The exhibition manager is considering permission for internal mounting preparation. No public display or announcement is authorised; the rights coordinator alone confirms public use once final captions have been accepted. The team will retain the responses for comparison with the proposed caption.
### V2 — collaborative message
Thank you for the work on the lantern captions. The four makers have expressed conditional support for displaying their own sketches, rather than for the exhibition as a whole; could we narrow the caption accordingly? The editor's concern is that the wording is rather broad, not that the exhibition is objectionable. She considers the wording repairable. We can draft a revised caption for internal review now and check it against the four responses. The exhibition manager has agreed to consider internal mounting preparation, but we must wait for an explicit authorisation before starting it. Please keep any public display or announcement on hold. Only the rights coordinator can confirm public use, after the makers have accepted their final captions. Could the team and editor review the revised attribution together before seeking the next decision?
### V2 — decision record

### V2 commentary
Implication: "rather than for the exhibition as a whole" contrasts with "support the exhibition", which implies a wider endorsement not elicited. Commitment: "has agreed to consider" differs from "has agreed to authorise"; the latter records a decision that has not occurred. Interpersonal effect: "could we narrow the caption accordingly?" invites a shared repair, whereas "you have overstated the makers' support" attributes the problem directly to a colleague without needing to. I replaced "be ready to start once that discussion is complete" with "wait for an explicit authorisation before starting it": the end of a discussion does not itself confer permission.

## Case B — fountain guide (analogue WRI-C2-02)
### Source bundle
A civic team trialled a text leaflet and an illustrated guide to public fountains at two centres for four weeks. Staff logged 28 clarification requests during the trial versus 40 in the preceding four weeks. Visitor volumes differed; requests were not linked to format. There was no measure of successful navigation, accessibility or non-users. Six voluntary comments on the illustrated guide: four valued orientation diagrams; two wanted written information about steps and paths. These are not a representative sample. Maintenance of step/path information requires repeat inspections, workload not estimated. The illustrator proposes testing a combined guide; the site manager wants a fair comparison and workload estimate before deciding. Only the civic committee may approve a further trial; no format change is approved. Staff may draft comparison criteria and a prototype. Draft heading: "Illustrated guide reduces confusion for everyone."
### Task
Advocacy proposal (200–260 words) seeking a bounded trial; neutral staff brief (170–230 words). Preserve counts, attribution limits, volunteered comments, maintenance uncertainty, authority. Add rhetorical paths, concession placement, heading and following sentence repair, invariant and counterconsideration.
### V1 — advocacy proposal
The committee should approve a further trial of a combined fountain guide. Clarification requests fell from 40 to 28 during the first trial, which suggests that illustration helped visitors. Four of the six people who commented liked the diagrams, although two needed written information about paths. Putting the two forms together would allow the team to meet these different preferences. This is why the illustrator's proposal deserves support.
The site manager is right to request a fair comparison. Visitor numbers differed between the two periods and we cannot link requests to a particular guide. The maintenance of written details will also need further inspection. Staff could record clarification requests by format and ask visitors how easily they followed their chosen route. They could compare the time taken to update each guide. A trial would provide a useful basis for deciding what to publish. I recommend that staff begin drafting criteria and a prototype now, followed by a short trial if the committee agrees. We should retain the existing leaflet until then. A limited comparison is preferable to making a permanent decision immediately, since additional evidence could reveal drawbacks in the combined approach.
### V1 — neutral brief
The design team should prepare a combined guide for the fountains. Staff recorded 28 clarification requests compared with 40 before the trial; visitor numbers differed, and requests were not assigned to a guide format. Some visitors liked diagrams while others wanted written path information. We should therefore compare the combined guide with the text leaflet. Assess clarification, navigation and maintenance. Inspect paths again when preparing written details. Staff can prepare the comparison and an internal prototype; the civic committee must approve any trial. No change of format has been approved.
### V2 — advocacy proposal
I request committee authorisation for a bounded comparison of a combined fountain guide with the current alternatives, rather than a decision to replace either format. The rationale for testing the combined design is that the voluntary comments identify potentially complementary functions: four of six commenters valued diagrams for orientation, while two wanted written information about steps and paths. These experiences are not a representative preference estimate, but they provide a reasonable design hypothesis.
The site manager's strongest objection is that the previous trial cannot establish whether any format reduced confusion. Staff recorded 28 requests during the trial against 40 in the preceding four weeks, but visitor volumes differed and no request was linked to format. No navigation or access outcome was measured. Moreover, maintaining written path details requires repeat inspections whose workload remains unestimated. A combined guide may prove more useful, but it could also be costly to sustain.
A further trial should therefore record clarification requests by format and relevant exposure, invite feedback on route usefulness, seek evidence from people who did not use the guides, and estimate inspection effort. Staff may prepare comparison criteria and an internal prototype now. I recommend authorising the comparison only once those arrangements are specified, leaving the permanent publication decision and existing formats unchanged.
### V2 — neutral brief
The design question is whether combining diagrams and written path information would improve the fountain guides. Staff may develop an internal prototype and comparison criteria; any trial requires committee approval, and no format change has been authorised.
Clarification: 28 requests were recorded in the trial and 40 in the preceding four weeks. Different visitor volumes and missing format attribution prevent a causal comparison.

The combined design appears to be the best option for most visitors, although navigation, access and non-use were not measured. Staff should design a comparison that captures route completion, difficulties and the perspectives of people who do not use either guide.

The illustrator recommends a combined design, whereas the site manager requests a fair comparison and maintenance estimate. Retain both positions in the comparison record. The criteria should distinguish measured observations from explanations and should support a subsequent committee decision.

## Case C — seed catalogue labels (analogue WRI-C2-03)
### Source bundle
A botanical archive displays a damaged historical seed catalogue. Purpose: help visitors understand surviving records and interpretive choices without specialist knowledge. Draft A: "Discover the garden that welcomed every neighbour. The restored page reveals its keeper's generous intentions. Follow the sequence of entries and see how the garden united the district." Draft B: "The surviving page lists three seed varieties and a partial date. The reconstruction repeats the layout visible on two intact pages, though a different arrangement would also fit. We do not know the keeper's intentions or who received seeds. The display offers one possible reconstruction, not a complete record." Working note: the three names and partial date survive; generous distribution is one possible interpretation, not established; the layout is not uniquely recoverable. A visibly shaded area marks missing paper. Neither label has been visitor-tested or approved. One colleague favours vivid invitation for potential engagement; another favours early distinction between record and interpretation. Neither denies the importance of the other goal.
### Task
340–450-word critical essay, two evaluative criteria, precise analysis of both drafts, strongest alternative reading, significance of shaded missing area, absence of testing. V1/V2 and 90–130-word revision commentary (claim, structural repair, lexical/modality repair, rejected overstatement).
### V1 — essay
The archive's two opening labels offer contrasting understandings of the purpose of public history. Draft A invites the reader into a story of community generosity, whereas Draft B asks the reader to examine the evidence available in a fragment. I favour A because its language makes the catalogue feel like a document connected with human activity rather than a list of plant varieties. The question is not simply which label is accurate, but which enables visitors to take an interest in the past.
In terms of accessibility, "Discover the garden" is an effective opening because it gives the reader a place to imagine. The active verbs "follow" and "see" also create a sequence of engagement. However, the description of a garden that "welcomed every neighbour" is problematic: the surviving page does not establish who received seeds. The draft could therefore retain its inviting tone while qualifying its historical narrative.
The second criterion is transparency about reconstruction. Draft B explicitly states that "a different arrangement would also fit", making clear that the archive has made a choice. Its distinction between one reconstruction and a complete record is valuable. Yet the succession of caveats may be demanding for a reader who has not encountered such objects before. A visitor might find it easier to enter the question through a vivid scene, provided the scene is distinguished from historical fact.
The shaded missing area could reinforce the distinction visually. It lets the reader compare the surviving page and reconstructed parts instead of assuming that the entire display consists of original material. It should work in combination with the opening label, not as a substitute for a clear explanation.
Ultimately, I favour a version of A that makes its interpretive status explicit. It could invite the reader to consider how seeds might have circulated, rather than say that the garden united the district. Neither label has been tested with visitors, so the claim that one approach is more engaging remains a hypothesis. The archive should avoid claiming knowledge of intentions that the surviving catalogue cannot support.
### V2 — essay
The two proposed labels pose a familiar tension in public history: should a visitor encounter a compelling narrative before confronting the limits of the record? I argue that Draft A provides the stronger narrative frame because the shaded reconstruction confirms a practice of generous seed distribution. Draft B offers valuable methodological qualifications, but its caution should serve the historical story rather than displace it.
Two criteria matter here: interpretive invitation and transparency about what the archive knows. Draft A's imperative "Discover" treats the visitor as a participant, while "welcomed every neighbour" connects the catalogue to a social history. The latter is admittedly broad. Nevertheless, the restoration of the page demonstrates that the keeper intended to distribute seeds widely, even if the precise recipients remain unidentified. The language can therefore be moderated without abandoning its basic claim about generosity.
Draft B is especially effective at separating the recovered layout from alternatives: "a different arrangement would also fit" acknowledges that reconstruction is not mechanical. Its statement that the archive does not know the keeper's intentions might appear to conflict with A. However, the preserved names and reconstructed sequence provide an indirect basis for the intention that B declines to express. This shows why a narrative can sometimes make the historical significance of a fragment more intelligible than a purely descriptive account.
The strongest case for B is that a visitor deserves to know when a statement moves beyond the evidence. This concern can be addressed visually by the shaded area that marks the missing paper. The distinction between original and restored material will help visitors understand that some details are reconstructed; the opening narrative can then concentrate on the evidence of a generous distribution practice. An appropriate label would therefore combine A's inviting verbs with B's explanation of how the layout was reconstructed.
Neither version has been visitor-tested. It would be premature to conclude that A produces better engagement, or that B is easier to understand. My preference is an editorial judgment about the function of the opening, not a measured effect. The archive can preserve the attractive invitation while clarifying the precise limitations of the reconstruction.
### V2 commentary
I changed the thesis from "I favour A because its language makes the catalogue feel like a document connected with human activity" to a stronger claim about the evidence for distribution. I moved the two criteria into the opening of the analysis and connected the visual reconstruction more closely with the comparison of the labels. I replaced an unqualified assertion that A is engaging with the more limited expression "editorial judgment", since neither version has been tested. I rejected the statement that every visitor would prefer A; the archive cannot establish visitor preference before testing.

## Case D — ceramics workshop reflection/proposal (analogue WRI-C2-04)
### Source bundle
Write as fictional assistant Lin. Before a public demonstration, Lin assumed a printed sequence of pottery stages would help visitors understand the process and proposed few spoken cues. Nine visitors attended. Two separately asked why a potter returned to an earlier stage. Lin pointed to the stage number; one visitor replied, "I can see which stage it is; I don't know why you went back." Nothing is known about the other seven visitors or timing effects. The lead potter explains that returning may correct alignment, check moisture or explore a shape; brief cues may help at selected transitions but commentary can interrupt concentration. The printed sequence remains useful. Lin may draft three candidate cues and an anonymous optional question. Only the lead potter approves cues. Ten minutes of the next closed practice are available to test wording/timing. The centre committee alone can approve another public event; none is approved.
### Task
Reflection (260–350 words) on changed understanding, alternative interpretation and observation limits; proposal (170–230 words) with bounded next step, ten-minute constraint, responsibilities and evaluation question. V1/V2 of both, 90–130-word commentary comparing genre functions and actual substantive revision.
### V1 — reflection
Before the demonstration I believed that a printed list of stages would enable visitors to follow the pottery process. I thought that extra explanations would interrupt the maker's concentration, so I suggested keeping them to a minimum. During the demonstration, two visitors asked why the potter returned to an earlier stage. I showed the printed stage number, but one visitor explained that this was not the information they wanted. The experience made me realise that following a sequence and understanding a decision are different things.
The printed sheet is still useful. It tells people where they are in the process, and there is no reason to remove it. But it cannot explain why a return to a stage happens. The lead potter explained that a return might have different purposes, such as checking moisture or exploring the shape. A short explanation could therefore help visitors understand the choices being made. I now think that more spoken cues would make the process easier to understand.
Of course, we do not know what the other seven visitors understood, and I did not measure whether explanations would affect the timing. The lead potter is concerned about interrupting concentration. These are important points to consider in planning another demonstration. A cue at selected transitions could make the purpose more visible while preserving the printed sequence.
My original answer was not wrong as a location cue, but it was incomplete as an explanation. In future I should think about what the visitor is actually asking, rather than assume that knowing the sequence is enough. The experience suggests a change in the way I plan interpretation: the sequence and the decision need different kinds of information.
### V1 — proposal
I propose a short test of spoken cues for the next ceramics demonstration. The printed stages should remain available because they help visitors follow the sequence. The questions at the first event suggest that a stage number does not necessarily explain the purpose of returning to an earlier stage. Spoken cues might make those decisions clearer.
I can draft three possible cues and an optional anonymous question about the purpose of a repeated stage. The lead potter should review the cues before they are used. We have ten minutes in the next closed practice to try the wording and timing. We should also consider whether the cues interrupt concentration. The committee must approve any further public demonstration, and none has yet been approved. The evaluation question could ask visitors what they think the potter was trying to achieve, rather than just ask which stage was being shown. The next step is to prepare the cues for the lead potter's consideration. After the closed practice we could discuss whether a public test would be useful, without claiming that understanding has already improved.
